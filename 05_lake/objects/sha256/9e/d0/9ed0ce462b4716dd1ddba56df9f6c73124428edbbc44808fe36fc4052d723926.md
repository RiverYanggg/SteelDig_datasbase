Microstructure and deformation behaviour of austenitic low-density steels: The defining role of B2 intermetallic phase

a b s t r a c t

[BLOCK b0009]
The feasibility of significant weight reduction in conjunction with the superior strength-ductility combination makes Fe-Mn-Al-C-based steels the candidate material for automotive and structural applications. The alloy chemistry and processing conditions influence the microstructure in low-density steels (LDS); however, their role in deformation behaviour is not fully understood. In the present study, three diferent austenitic LDS grades viz. Fe-28Mn-9Al-0.9C, Fe-28Mn-9Al-5Ni-0.9C and Fe-15Mn-9Al-5Ni-0.9C alloys in hot-rolled condition were used for evaluating the role of secondary phases, especially B2 ordered Fe(Ni)Al phase on deformation characteristics. Ni-free LDS microstructure consisted of the ??-matrix with fine ??-carbides, whereas Ni containing low Mn alloy possessed coarser ??-carbides along with B2 in both bamboo-like stringer and polygonal particle morphology in the γ-matrix. The B2 containing alloy exhibited higher strength with reduced tensile ductility than the B2-free alloy. Hardness of the ??-matrix and B2 phase were similar with B2 grains exhibiting distinct pop-in events in the nanoindentation curves indicating incipient plasticity. The ??-matrix and B2 co-deform near yield, and a favourable orientation relationship (OR) between the ??-matrix and B2 facilitated easy slip transfer, while the non-favourable OR controlled ductility by strain accumulation and B2-cracking. The diferences in the strain hardening behaviour of B2-free and B2 containing alloys were elucidated based on the changes in dislocation substructure evolution examined by an automated crystal orientation mapping in electron microscopy.

[BLOCK b0010]
1. Introduction

[BLOCK b0011]
The demand for lightweight, high-strength structural materials has seen an exponential rise in recent times. The reduction in overall weight has become an essential criterion for several applications, especially in automotive and aerospace sectors, with a thrust to improve fuel eficiency and bring down emission levels. There have been significant developments in producing advanced high strength steels (AHSS) exhibiting a reasonable ductility necessary for forming various structural components. In this context, at present, there exists three generations of AHSS [1–3]. The 3rd generation AHSS are still under the nascent stage of research, whereas the 2nd generation AHSS have already gained importance to replace the 1st generation steels in specific structural components. Amongst the 2nd generation AHSS, Fe-Mn-Al-C based steel grades are popularly termed as either low-density steels (LDS) or lightweight steels with induced plasticity (L-IP). These alloys ofer superior mechanical properties and extraordinary weight reductions because of alloy chemistry and complex strengthening mechanisms [4–6]. Based on the constitution of phases, LDS are classified as ferritic, austenitic, duplex, or triplex. Austenite (??) based LDS alloys have four major microstructural features: (i) the ??-matrix, (ii) $\mathbf { L } 1 _ { 2 }$ ordered $\mathrm { ( F e M n ) } _ { 3 } \mathrm { A l C }$ (??-carbide) particles, (iii) B2 ordered Fe(Ni)Al type intermetallic compounds, and (iv) $\mathrm { D O } _ { 3 }$ ordered $\mathsf { F e } _ { 3 } \mathbf { A l }$ precipitates [7–9]. The formation of these features depends entirely on alloy chemistry and thermomechanical processing conditions. Based on the size, shape, and site of occurrence, the morphology of B2 can be classified as (i) elongated cylindrical shapes called stringers which exist across several ??-grains, (ii) intergranular spherical (200 nm – 1 ??m) at the recrystallised ??-grain boundaries, and (iii) intragranular precipitates (50 – 300 nm) formed within the shear bands in the deformed ??-grains [10]. It has also been reported that nickel addition would improve the strength of LDS alloys through the formation of Fe(Ni)Al type B2 phase [10].

[BLOCK b0012]
Exhaustive literature is available on deformation behaviour and microstructural evolution in B2-free LDS [11–20]. However, limited studies exist which focus on understanding the influence of B2, with contrasting observations, on deformation behaviour of B2-containing LDS [21,22]. These studies involved the tensile deformation behaviour of cold-rolled plus annealed B2-containing LDS alloys. Yang et al.

[BLOCK b0013]
[21] have reported that B2 plays a significant role in deformation response. Strain incompatibility arises due to microstructural heterogeneity, leading to increased back stress in the ??-matrix. Both the back stress-induced hardening and forest dislocation hardening contribute to the high strain hardening ability of the alloy. On the contrary, Kim [22] showed experimentally and argued that B2 has a limited role in the deformation response of LDS alloys. It was reported that the ??-matrix composition plays a dominant role in the strain hardening. In addition, it was documented that the B2 phase in stringer morphology can afect forming by reducing ductility in transverse direction [23]. It is worth mentioning that the introduction of cold rolling followed by annealing heat treatment modifies the stringer orientation and its characteristics [10,24–26].

[BLOCK b0014]
From the industrial processing point of view, the as-cast ingots/slabs undergo hot rolling practice before cold rolling (and annealing) to produce required sheets for forming any structural component. Thus, there is a necessity for understanding the efect of alloy chemistry on the microstructural evolution from casting to hot rolling and subsequently the role of the constituents on deformation behaviour of the hot-rolled LDS alloys before cold rolling. However, there are limited studies on deformation behaviour and the evolution of deformed microstructure in the hot-rolled LDS. This literature gap highlights the need for a systematic investigation to evaluate the alloy chemistry and the role of the resultant secondary phases such as ??-carbide, B2 stringers/particles on deformation behaviour of hot-rolled Fe-Mn-Al-C-(Ni) based LDS alloys.

[BLOCK b0015]
In view of the aforementioned literature gaps, the present study was undertaken with specific objective of investigating the role of microstructural constituents, the B2 phase in particular, on the strengthductility combination observed in the as-hot rolled plates of Ni-free and Ni containing LDS. Three low-density steel alloy compositions, which are known to produce B2-free and B2 containing ??-matrix, such as 28Mn0Ni, 28Mn5Ni, and 15Mn5Ni alloys with nearly constant Al and C contents, were melted, and the ingots were subsequently hot-rolled. The mechanical properties were evaluated through hardness (macro & nanoindentation) and tensile testing at room temperature on the hotrolled alloys. The role of secondary phases on the deformation characteristics of LDS was deconvoluted by conducting detailed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) in conjunction with automated crystal orientation mapping (ACOM).

2. Materials and experimental procedure

[BLOCK b0017]
2.1. Materials

[BLOCK b0018]
For the present study, three diferent austenitic LDS grades were prepared by vacuum induction melting route. The defect-free portions of the 30 kg ingots were homogenised at 1423 K for two hours. The chemical composition, analysed by wet chemical method/standard emission spectroscopy, of the homogenised ingots are provided in Table 1. The three LDS grades: Fe-28Mn-9Al-0.9C, Fe-28Mn-9Al-5Ni-0.9C, and Fe-15Mn-9Al-5Ni-0.9C are hereafter referred to as 28Mn0Ni, 28Mn5Ni, and 15Mn5Ni, respectively. The concentration of Mn and Ni content were selected to obtain B2-free and B2-containing alloys based on stability of austenite. Two diferent contents of Mn and Ni each were selected based on the stability of austenite to produce B2-free and B2-containing microstructures. Hot rolling of the homogenised ingots was carried out at a temperature of 1323 K. Total thickness reduction of nearly 85% was achieved with ∼15% reduction per pass.

[BLOCK b0021]
2.2. Mechanical characterization

[BLOCK b0022]
Macrohardness measurements were performed on the hot-rolled samples using Vickers indenter under a peak load of 300 N, as per ASTM E140–02 standards. The average of ten hardness readings taken at random locations on each sample was reported. Localised nanoindentation measurements were performed on the austenite matrix and B2 grains of the as-rolled samples at a peak load of 5 mN under con stant loading/unloading rates of $5 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { N } ~ s ^ { \mathrm { ~ - ~ } 1 }$ and a dwell time of 5 s. To this end, EBSD is performed on a fiducially marked region followed by nanoindentation on select B2 and ?? grains. The hardness and the reduced modulus of both phases were determined from load versus depth of penetration curves using the Oliver-Pharr model [27]. The average of measurements of nearly 50 grains of each phase was reported.

[BLOCK b0023]
Cylindrical specimens with a gauge diameter of 6 mm and gauge length of 30 mm were prepared as per ASTM E8M standard for evaluating the tensile behaviour of the hot-rolled alloys. The tensile specimens were cut along the rolling direction (RD), with a few selected samples of 15Mn5Ni alloy cut along the transverse direction (TD) to investigate the in-plane tensile anisotropy. The tensile tests were carried out at room temperature and initial strain rate of $1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \textbf { s } ^ { - \mathrm { ~ 1 ~ } }$ using LF200 Walter+Bai AG testing machine. The tensile elongation was measured using both punch marks and an extensometer on the gauge portion. Three samples were tested for each condition for reproducibility. Additionally, interrupted tensile tests were conducted on selected specimens to assess the microstructural evolution as a function of strain.

[BLOCK b0024]
2.3. Microstructural characterization

[BLOCK b0025]
Microstructural observations were carried out using an Olympus optical microscope. Electron channelling contrast imaging (ECCI) and orientation imaging microscopy (OIM) were carried out on an FEI scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with an EDAX electron backscattered difraction (EBSD) detector. The samples were prepared by polishing on abrasive papers followed by final vibratory polishing with colloidal silica. Initially, large area EBSD scans of $5 0 0 \times 5 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }$ were obtained with a step size of $0 . 2 \mu \mathrm { m }$ . Additional high-resolution scans were performed at an area of $1 0 0 \times 1 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }$ with a step size of 0.1 ??m to resolve fine microstructural features. The OIM data was post-processed using EDAX TSL-OIM v8 software for evaluating grain orientations and phase fractions. The microstructures of the hot-rolled samples were examined along the RD-ND plane at the mid-section. The grain size was calculated using the linear intercept method with twin boundaries included during the analysis. Σ3 boundaries based on the coincidence site lattice (CSL) model were used to analyse twin boundary fractions in the microstructure. Twin boundaries were included in grain size calculations. The recrystallization fraction was estimated using the grain orientation spread (GOS) parameter. A critical value of $2 ^ { \circ }$ was chosen as the GOS cut-of for determining the recrystallised grain fraction. The composition of various phases was analysed by energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). Additionally, X-ray difraction (XRD) was performed on the hot-rolled samples using cobalt $\mathrm { K } _ { \alpha }$ radiation at 135 mA current and 40 kV voltage in Rigaku smart lab X-ray difractometer.

[BLOCK b0026]
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) studies were carried out on the hot-rolled and selected deformed tensile samples to examine the detailed microstructural features. Infrared thermography coupled with the tensile test was employed to identify the deformed region during tensile testing for TEM sample preparation. TEM sample preparation was initially done by mechanical polishing down to ∼100 ??m thickness. This was followed by twin jet electropolishing using an electrolyte consisting of nitric acid, hydrofluoric acid, sulfuric acid and lactic acid in methanol at 243 K at 18 V. Automated crystal orientation mapping (ACOM) was performed in TEM using Nanomegas ASTAR hardware and software packages. A precession angle of $0 . 5 ^ { \circ }$ was used for ACOM measurements, and the area of interest was scanned with a step size of 10 nm. The elec tron microscopy studies were performed on a Tecnai 20T G2 (FEI) TEM operated at 200 kV. In addition, fractography studies were carried out using a Zeiss EVO 18 SEM on the failed tensile specimens.

3.3. Post-tensile deformed microstructures

[BLOCK b0048]
Fig. 6 shows the fracture surfaces of the specimens of the three al loys after tensile testing. The 15Mn5Ni alloy showed a distinctive fracture surface topography from that of the other two alloys. The insets in each image show one-half of the failed tensile specimens. Both 28Mn0Ni and 28Mn5Ni alloys exhibit clear cup and cone type failure (insets), as evident from the dimples. This suggests ductile failure involving microvoid formation, growth and coalescence. The 15Mn5Ni alloy specimen displayed numerous micro-cleavage facets connected by dimples on a nearly flat surface at the macroscale (Fig. 6c). This suggests that the fracture in 15Mn5Ni alloy is crack initiation-controlled, triggered by the B2 stringers. Further microstructural analysis was done to ascertain the role of B2 on the strength of the alloy and changes in the fracture behaviour.

[BLOCK b0049]
Transmission electron microscopy was performed on 28Mn0Ni and 15Mn5Ni alloy samples to deconvolute the efect of microstructural constituents on yield strength, strain hardening and ductility. Specimens were deformed to true strains of ∼0.015 and ∼0.15 in tension, representing the two diferent strain hardening regimes observed in the alloys (Fig. 5b). Fig. 7 shows TEM micrographs of 28Mn0Ni and 15Mn5Ni alloys deformed to ∼0.015 strain. Fig. 7a shows dislocation pinning in the deformed 28Mn0Ni alloy. For the same applied strain, the 15Mn5Ni alloy showed significant dislocation accumulation in the austenite channels in between the ??-carbide particles, as seen in Fig. 7b. Both the ??- matrix and B2 stringers underwent significant deformation as evident from the dislocation activity. In the present study involving similar processing conditions, finer ??- carbides are seen in both 28Mn0Ni and 28Mn5Ni alloys, whereas the 15Mn5Ni alloy exhibited relatively coarser ??-carbides in cuboidal morphology. Nevertheless, this demands a detailed study on evolution of various phases and their morphology as a function of alloying content and processing conditions using thermodynamic and kinetics modelling and it is beyond the scope of the present study.

4.2.2. Strength

[BLOCK b0070]
The major factors which contribute to the overall strength of LDS alloys are (i) strength of both the recrystallised and the deformed austenite, (ii) strengthening due to ??-carbides, (iii) strengthening due to B2 phase (and $\mathrm { D O } _ { 3 }$ precipitates if any), and (iv) strain partitioning between the austenite and B2 stringers/particles. The yield strength of the recrystallised austenite can be calculated using the following expression [28,33]:

[BLOCK b0071]
$$
\sigma_ {y, R x - a u s t} = \sigma_ {0} + k _ {H P} d ^ {- 1 / 2}
$$

[BLOCK b0072]
Where $\sigma _ { O }$ is lattice frictional stress, d is the average recrystallised grain size, and $k _ { H P }$ is the Hall-Petch coeficient. The values of $\sigma _ { 0 }$ and ${ \mathrm { K } } _ { \mathrm { H P } }$ were determined from the experimental results reported by Etienne et al. [33] for Fe-28.8Mn-9.4Al-0.92C and are 288 MPa and 461 MPa $\mu \mathrm { m } ^ { 1 / 2 }$ \*5 respectively. The estimated yield strength of the recrystallised austenite having a grain size in the range of ∼5–7 ??m, as in present LDS alloys, is about 460–500 MPa. This value is about 170–200 MPa less than that of the experimentally observed yield strength of the 28Mn0Ni alloy. Additionally, the yield strength of 28Mn0Ni alloy is higher than those reported for alloys of similar composition under cold rolled plus annealed conditions [11,12,33]. This diference can be attributed to the volume fraction of ??-carbides.

[BLOCK b0073]
Nanoindentation study reveals that both B2 and ??-matrix exhibit similar hardness values in the range of 6.7–6.8 GPa (Table 3). Rahnama et al. [25] investigated the nanomechanical response of the individual or the combined precipitates depending on their size along with the ??-matrix in Fe-15Mn-10Al-0.8C-(0,5)Ni LDS alloys. They reported the hardness values for ??+??, ??+B2, ??+??+B2, and B2 stringer as ∼7 GPa, ∼8 GPa, ∼9 GPa and ∼9–11 GPa, respectively. The average hardness of ??+?? in the present LDS alloy is close to those reported by Rahnama et al. [25]. It is important to note that the B2 phase can exhibit a wide range of hardness due to the diferences in nature of bonds such as Fe-Al and Ni-Al in Ni-free and Ni containing LDS alloys, respectively, and variation in the annealing/rolling temperatures. The similar hardness values of the ??-matrix and B2 in the present study implies that both ?? and B2 phases can deform simultaneously. However, the loaddepth curves of B2 stringer displayed a characteristic ‘pop-in’ event in the loading segment (inset in Fig. 4b). A pop-in event involves a sudden burst in displacement during the load-controlled experiment. These pop-in events were notably absent during the indentation of ??-grains. Pop-in events are reported to be linked to incipient plasticity, where an elastic-plastic transition occurs beneath the indenter [34]. The sharp bursts of activity result from thermally activated deformation processes such as dislocation nucleation or release of pinned dislocations. A recent study on the nanoindentation of dual-phase steel under diferent heat treatments highlighted the efect of carbon difusion-induced dislocation pinning, leading to similar pop-in behaviour as observed in the current study [35]. The possibility of pop-ins resulting from the release of pinned dislocations thus presents an interesting opportunity for study as the compositional diferences between B2 and austenite were quantified without carbon. Also, presence of $\mathrm { D O } _ { 3 }$ phase was also observed in the B2 stringers (Fig. 3d). This diference in carbon content or the DO phase in B2 could contribute to the initial dislocation pinning. Li et al. [36] investigated the pop-in nature in B2 (NiAl) single crystals and reported strong crystallographic dependence of the first pop-in loads with lowest for ⟨111⟩ and highest for ⟨001⟩ orientations. Hence, the range of first pop-in loads (∼50–200 μN) noticed in B2 grains (Fig. 4b) could be a resultant of the orientation dependence. Currently, this is out of the scope of this work and it requires further experiments for validation.

[BLOCK b0074]
The evidence of slip transfer occurring across favourable orientations (Fig. 7c) in 15Mn5Ni confirms both the ??-matrix and B2 would deform together as composite yielding occurs. The higher yield strength of 15Mn5Ni alloy as compared to 28Mn0Ni (and 28Mn5Ni) can be attributed to (i) the presence of a large fraction of relatively coarser ??- carbides within the ??-matrix (Fig. 3g), (ii) a bamboo-like grain morphology of B2 stringers containing fine $\mathrm { D O } _ { 3 }$ ordered precipitates and (iii) the restricted slip activity across non-favourable $\gamma / \mathrm { { B } } 2$ interfaces. The increase in ?? particles size and fraction changes the deformation mechanism from weakly coupled particle shearing to strongly coupled one and eventually to Orowan looping, as suggested by Yao et al. [31]. This transition in mechanisms can be expected in an alloy when the size of ??-carbides is ∼20 nm [31]. With a wide size distribution of ?? particles in the range of 5–40 nm in the present LDS alloys, either particle pining of dislocations (Fig. 7a), shearing (Fig. 8c) or looping (Fig. 7b) can be expected (Fig. 7b). Non-shearable B2 particles can also increase the strength of the alloy further due to dislocation pile-up around them (Figs. 7h, 8e & 9f) [10,28].

[BLOCK b0075]
4.2.3. Strain hardening and ductility

[BLOCK b0076]
The role of B2 phase on the strain hardening behaviour of LDS alloys has become a debatable topic in the recent past, and as highlighted in introduction section there exists two opposing view-points [21][22]. The mechanism proposed by Yang et al. [21] was based on the strain and stress partitioning between ?? and B2 phases. They reported that deformation of B2 phase starts at a later stage of deformation $( \varepsilon \sim 0 . 0 8 )$ but strain hardens faster than ?? phase. The transient plateau in the flow curve was thus attributed to this composite deformation. Subsequent back stress generation and forest hardening in the ??-matrix in the presence of B2 led to higher hardening rate observed in the B2 containing alloy. On the contrary, Kim [22] emphasised that the dislocation substructure evolution in austenite is governed by alloy composition, which plays a primary role, and B2 has a secondary role in strain hardening. Since there was no plateau in the stress-strain curve, Kim [22] also proposed that the transient portion of the flow curve does not originate due to the stress/strain partitioning; rather, it may be an indication of Lüder band formation. The absence of any noticeable plateaus in the stress-strain curves of all the three alloys (Fig. 5a) supports the argu ment given by Kim [22].

[BLOCK b0077]
The slip activity in LDS alloy depends on the nature of ??/B2 interface. Favourably orientated soft B2 grains (with ??-grains) present in stringers can deform simultaneously through slip transfer near yield, whereas, slip transfer restriction across non-favourable $\gamma / \mathrm { { B } } 2$ interface increases the yield strength and afects subsequent strain hardening behaviour. It was observed that the strain hardening of both the alloys 28Mn0Ni and 15Mn5Ni mostly proceeds by planar glide and associated slip band refinement (Fig. 8). Similar dynamic slip band refinement has been reported in recent studies on B2-free alloys [18,20,31]. Planar glide of dislocation in B2-free alloys can be attributed to the glide plane softening caused by either shearing of ??-carbides or interaction between dislocations along with short-range order (SRO) [18,20,37]. The slip band spacing measured in the present study at a particular strain in 28Mn0Ni alloy is comparable to that reported by Welsch et al. [20]. Although the deformation substructure evolution and associated strain hardening have been reported for B2-free alloy, the same in the ??-matrix in presence of B2 has not been studied in-detail. The slip band spacing measured at a given strain (?? ∼ 0.15) in 28Mn0Ni alloy is ∼100–200 nm whereas in 15Mn5Ni alloy it is ∼400–600 nm. Moreover, the slip bands are homogeneously distributed in the 28Mn0Ni alloy as compared to in 15Mn5Ni alloy.

[BLOCK b0078]
Slip band nucleation occurs when the critical resolved shear stress exceeds the overall stress required to generate a Frank-Read source for a given loop length and the friction stress. Once a slip band nucleates, there will be a significant increase in dislocation density due to restricted cross-slip activity. With further deformation, more dislocations can pileup within bands, exhaust the active source due to the force equilibrium reached between the applied stress and the mutual repulsive stress between dislocations. A new source must be initiated to accommodate further strain, thereby continuing the deformation process. This process leads to dynamic slip band refinement. The slip band spacing also de pends on the long-range stress field between bands. The nature of these stress fields is governed by the number of dislocations piled-up within a band [38]. The presence of coherent precipitates in the matrix can also afect the stress fields and subsequently influence the microstructural evolution according to their size and distribution. Kuhlmann-Wilsdorf proposed that the dislocations are anchored in the presence of precipitates, resulting in a decrease in dislocation loop length which reduces the number of active sources at given stress and thereby afecting the slip band spacing [39]. Hence, it is expected that the slip bands can be refined at a slower rate in the presence of non-shearable particles, whereas shearable particles can enhance the slip band refinement rate. The current study observed ??-carbides in 28Mn0Ni alloy to be smaller and lesser in volume fraction and are sheared early during deformation. This resulted in the observed finer slip band spacing as compared to the 15Mn5Ni alloy. It can be pointed out that the ??-carbides in 15Mn5Ni alloy are not fully sheared across the specimen at low strain due to their inhomogeneous distribution which result in non-uniform slip band spacing. Apart from the ??-carbides, non-shearable B2 particles in the 15Mn5Ni alloy can also afect the slip band refinement. In the presence of B2, the active slip length reduced further, thereby leading to high yield stress and contributing to a slower rate of slip band refinement. Thus, the overall slip band spacing in 15Mn5Ni alloy decreases at a much slower rate, leading to a lower strain hardening rate than in 28Mn0Ni alloy. Apart from dynamic slip band refinement, annealing twin-slip interaction is also prominent in 28Mn0Ni alloy. Thus, the differences in strain-hardening characteristics of 28Mn0Ni and 15Mn5Ni alloy can be attributed to various initial microstructural features such as ??-carbides (its size and volume fraction), B2 (type, morphology and orientation relationship with the matrix) and subsequent dislocation interactions in the presence of these phases during deformation.

[BLOCK b0079]
It is well-understood that the strain hardening exponent (n) is a measure of uniform strain that a polycrystalline material can accommodate. As noted in Fig. 5b, 15Mn5Ni alloy displayed a lower $\cdot _ { n } \cdot$ value than 28Mn(0,5)Ni alloys, resulting in diferent ductility levels. The fracture surface of the failed tensile specimen of 15Mn5Ni alloy shows cleavage facets (in some B2 phase) connected by dimple morphology (Fig. 6c) and is consistent with that reported by Park et al. [23]. The microstructure of the failed specimen also showed cracks in B2 stringers (Fig. 10a,b), implying that the slip transfer at the $\gamma / \mathrm { { B } } 2$ interface plays a vital role in the composite deformation behaviour of 15Mn5Ni alloy. The deformation behaviour is also influenced by grain-to-grain slip transfer within the polycrystalline B2 stringer, as illustrated schematically in Fig. 11eh. There will be substantial strain accumulation around hard orientated B2 while an easy slip transfer occurs in the case of soft orientation. Furthermore, the soft orientated B2 deforms in conjunction with the adjacent matrix and its texture evolves to maintain the orientation relationship. The strain accumulation observed around the neighbouring hardorientated B2 results in deformation heterogeneity (Fig. 7h and Fig. 9f) and subsequent crack nucleation in B2 (Fig. 10). On further straining, the inhibition of slip transfer near $\gamma / \mathrm { { B } } 2$ interfaces leads to higher local misorientation within a ?? grain. These re-orientated regions can also facilitate deformation twinning (Fig. 8f) due to the medium SFE of the alloy as reported by Abedi et al. [40]. In summary, the nature of $\gamma / \mathrm { { B } } 2$ interfaces (non-favourable OR) and dislocation accommodation within ?? and B2 influence the strain hardening and the overall ductility of LDS alloys.

5. Conclusions

[BLOCK b0081]
Three diferent LDS grades viz. Fe-28Mn-9Al-0.9C, Fe-28Mn-9Al-5Ni-0.9C and Fe-15Mn-9Al-5Ni-0.9C, were produced by vacuum induction melting and subsequently hot-rolled. Microstructures of all alloys consisted of a ??-matrix containing ??-carbides. An additional B2 phase seen to form in Ni containing LDS alloys. B2 was present in the form of long polycrystalline stringers and fine polygonal particles. The present study aimed to understand the role of B2 on the tensile response and microstructural evolution of these alloys. The key findings from the present study are as follows:

[BLOCK b0082]
<sup>•</sup> Rich B2 phase containing alloy exhibited the highest strength with reduced ductility compared to the other alloys. The ??/B2 interface played a critical role in controlling the strength and ductility of B2 containing alloys. A favourable orientation relationship (OR) between the ??-matrix and B2 facilitated slip transfer while the nonfavourable OR controlled the ductility by strain accumulation and B2-cracking.

[BLOCK b0083]
<sup>•</sup> Localised nanoindentation of ??-matrix and grains in B2 stringer showed similar indentation response with B2 having a characteristic ‘pop-in’. This implies that during yield, co-deformation of ?? and B2 occurs along with slip transfer across favourable ??/B2 interfaces as confirmed by transmission electron microscopy.

[BLOCK b0084]
<sup>•</sup> The presence of both ??-carbides and B2 modified the dislocation substructure evolution in austenite during deformation. Dislocations were accumulated around ?? channels during yielding, which evolved further as slip bands at higher strains followed by dynamic slip band refinement. Slip band refinement in B2-free alloy was much faster with uniform slip band spacing of 100–200 nm, whereas the presence of B2 limited the slip band refinement. This led to coarse and inhomogeneous slip bands with spacing of 400–600 nm in 15Mn5N alloy.

[BLOCK b0085]
<sup>•</sup> At later stages of deformation, slip restriction at ??/B2 interface resulted in high local re-orientation within a ??-grain surrounded by B2 particles/stringers in 15Mn5Ni alloy as compared to the B2-free 28Mn0Ni alloy. Deformation twinning was also noticed in such reorientated grains which was absent in the B2-free 28Mn0Ni alloy.

[BLOCK b0086]
The present study’s findings provide a critical understanding to evaluate microstructural modifications to enhance the mechanical properties of B2 containing low density steels.
