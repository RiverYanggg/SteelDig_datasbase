Abstract Under equivalent stiffness conditions, material substitution based on a thin-walled design is crucial for the lightweight of components. Developing high-performance steels with both high-yield strength and excellent ductility has become a key focus in fields like aerospace and low altitude flight. The novel low-density steel presented here exhibits a yield strength of 1 000 MPa, which is 2–3 times higher than conventional low-alloy high-strength steels, while maintaining an elongation of about 18.7%. By combining ex-situ experimental characterization with a phase mechanical response model based on the iso-work theory and the von Mises equivalent method, the role of heterogeneous deformation-induced strengthening was revealed. The calculated values align closely with experimental results. This exceptional performance is attributed to a multiphase heterogeneous microstructure, where fresh martensite, bainite/tempered martensite, and deformation-induced martensite act as hard regions. These regions release micro-stresses through inhomogeneous cooperative deformation with soft ferrite, enabling multiple plastic deformation mechanisms and stress concentration relief. This research offers new insights into optimizing microstructures through mechanical metallurgy, which is crucial for producing high-performance, lightweight components.

[BLOCK b0012]
1. Introduction

[BLOCK b0013]
Energy conservation and emission reduction are essential responsibilities for modern industry and crucial pathways toward global sustainable development. Lightweight, one of the most effective strategies for achieving these goals, is partic ularly relevant in aerospace and low-altitude flight sectors, where the use of advanced high-strength steels has become a key trend. Studies show that reducing the total weight of airframes by 10% can enhance energy efficiency by 6%–8%.<sup>1,2</sup> In this context, researchers are focused on developing thirdgeneration advanced high-strength steels, such as mediummanganese steels and Quenching & Partitioning (Q&P) steels, due to their exceptional strength-ductility balance.<sup>3,4</sup> These materials have gained significant industrial attention for their potential to meet lightweight demands. The microstructure of Q&P steels typically consists of a martensitic matrix with a controlled amount of metastable austenite. The martensite provides high strength, while the metastable austenite enhances work-hardening capacity through the Transformation-Induced Plasticity (TRIP) effect during deformation. However, increasing the carbon content to improve strength compromises weldability, and raising the manganese content narrows the Q&P process window, making it harder to maintain product stability. These trade-offs complicate efforts to improve the comprehensive mechanical properties of Q&P steels. Medium-manganese steels (4wt%–12wt% Mn) feature a dual-phase microstructure of ferrite and metastable austenite, with a Product of Strength and Elongation (PSE) reaching up to 30–40 GPa %.<sup>5,6</sup> However, the softer ferritic matrix leads to lower yield strength, and the high manganese content causes microsegregation in cast ingots. Additionally, martensite formation during hot rolling complicates the production of cold-rolled products, posing further challenges for industrial-scale manufacturing. A pressing challenge lies in developing a structural material substitution method that maintains strength and ductility while fulfilling the demands of lightweight applications.

[BLOCK b0014]
Micro-defects that develop in structural materials during service can cause significant local stress concentrations, leading to catastrophic failures and substantial losses of life and property.<sup>7–10</sup> Studies show that reducing weight by 100 kg in structures like aircraft fuselage skins, engine casings, carrier frames, and crash beams increases safety risks by 4%–6%.<sup>11</sup> Therefore, it is imperative to develop new microstructural designs that effectively mitigate these harmful effects while meeting safety requirements and achieving lightweight construction. Under external stress, crystal defects and interfaces can form micropores, which subsequently expand and link into microcracks.<sup>12–14</sup> The significant stress concentration at crack tips makes grain boundaries prone to tearing, gradually leading to macroscopic fractures.<sup>15,16</sup> To address this issue, Xin et al.<sup>17</sup> proposed introducing an appropriate number of grain boundaries to alleviate stress concentration-induced damage. While this method contributes to balancing material strength and ductility, it is not markedly effective in suppressing stress concentration and crack propagation. This indicates a need to shift from a single-plane strain fracture mode to a multipleplane stress fracture mode, where the formation of multiple microcracks can absorb substantial fracture energy. For instance, Huang et al.<sup>18</sup> introduced high-density dislocations into martensite and combined them with weaker grain boundary interfaces to slow crack propagation, significantly enhancing the fracture toughness of the material. However, this strategy imposes excessively high demands on the dislocation storage capacity of material and is suitable primarily for situations involving sudden stress changes. Considering the load conditions, to address the issues of material substitution and lightweight in structural components during long-term service, it is especially important to start with the design of extensive dislocation interactions and accumulation.

[BLOCK b0015]
Layered heterogeneous structures across multiple scales are widely recognized for effectively regulating dislocation behavior, which enhances plasticity and strengthens materials by impeding dislocation movement.<sup>19–23</sup> This coupling effect is primarily due to Heterogeneous Deformation-Induced (HDI) strengthening, driven by interactions between adjacent strain fields during deformation.<sup>24</sup> Accumulating Geometrically Necessary Dislocations (GNDs) at interfaces distributes local plastic strain more uniformly, reducing stress concentrations and improving strength and toughness.<sup>25–29</sup> However, forming multiscale heterogeneous structures remains challenging in large-scale production. Leveraging key insights and following the principle of ‘material simplification’, we developed novel low-density steel that eliminates costly elements like Nb, Mo, and Ni, while slightly increasing Al and Mn content. By using mechanical-metallurgical methods that are easily applicable to actual production, a multi-level microstructure is introduced into this steel. The various mechanical responses promote the coupling of multiple strengthening mechanisms, forming strain gradients that effectively transmit stress and alleviate stress concentration. This material significantly outperforms conventional high-strength low-alloy steel by enhancing strength while maintaining good ductility, opening new avenues for lightweight structural design and innovative manufacturing of high-performance large components.

[BLOCK b0017]
2.1. Specimen preparation

[BLOCK b0018]
A 39 kg low-density steel ingot, with the composition Fe-0.2 5C-3Mn-2Al-0.4Si-0.009S-0.009P, was produced via vacuum induction melting. The ingot was hot forged and cut into billets measuring 90 mm (length) 70 mm (width) 40 mm (thickness). These billets were heated to 1 200 °??C and held for 60 min. Hot rolling was carried out on a high-rigidity two-roll mill, beginning at $1 \ 1 0 0 \ { } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ The process involved six passes with a cumulative reduction rate of 90%, yielding plates with a final thickness of 4 mm. Finish rolling was completed at 880 °??C, followed by air cooling. Cylindrical specimens (10 mm (length) 3 mm (diameter)) were extracted from the hot-rolled plates for phase transformation studies using dilatometry, with the critical temperature range illustrated in Fig. A1 in Appendix A. Cold rolling was performed on a four-roll reversible mill with a 60% reduction rate, resulting in plates with a thickness of 1.6 mm. The cold-rolled samples were annealed at 750, 800, 850 °??C for 30 min within the critical region and air-cooled to room temperature, labeled as AN750, AN800, and AN850, respectively. For further treatment, the samples underwent low bainite aging by holding at 400 °??C for 30 min, producing samples designated as AN750-T, AN800-T, and AN850-T. The full process flow is illustrated in Fig. 1(a).

[BLOCK b0019]
2.2. Microstructure characterization

[BLOCK b0020]
The phase composition and microstructure of the samples were analyzed using a field emission Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) and Electron Backscatter Diffraction (EBSD) system (EBSD-FEI Quanta-650 FEG). A 4% nitric acid ethanol solution was used for etching, while the electrolyte for electropolishing consisted of 80% ethanol, 15% perchloric acid, and 5% glycerol. Electropolishing was conducted at 28 V and 0.1 A for 15–30 s. EBSD analysis was conducted with a 20 kV operating voltage and a scanning step size of 0.2 <sup>l</sup>m. The data was analyzed using TSL OIM 7.0, indexing both Body-Centered Cubic (BCC) and Face-Centered Cubic (FCC) phases. A minimum pixel size of 9 was applied to minimize the risk of mis-indexed pixels being mistaken for real grains.<sup>30</sup> Dislocation and substructure evolution were examined with a Tecnai G2 F20 transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) at 200 kV. Elemental distribution within the microstructure was determined using a JEOL JXA-8230 Electron Probe Microanalyzer (EPMA) at 15 kV with a working current of $5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 8 }$ A. X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) was used to identify the phases in the microstructure. XRD phase analysis was performed on the rolling plane of the sample with an accelerating voltage of 40 kV, a tube current of 40 mA, and a scanning angle range of 30°??–110°??.

3. Results

[BLOCK b0026]
Fig. 2 presents SEM photographs illustrating the evolution of multiphase microstructures during rolling and heat treatment. The hot-rolled microstructure primarily consists of Bainite (B) with a small amount of Fresh Martensite (FM). The phase distribution is shown in Fig. 3(a), consisting entirely of a BCC structure. After cold rolling, the bainite exhibits an elongated morphology aligned with the rolling direction and is interspersed with minor amounts of fresh martensite. During intercritical annealing, portions of the bainite and martensite reaustenitize due to the high solubility of carbon in austenite, which leads to carbon enrichment and enhanced stability within the austenite. Carbon-depleted regions form Ferrite (F), while some carbon-enriched austenite is retained during cooling, becoming Retained Austenite (RA). Unstabilized austenite undergoes a diffusionless transformation during cooling to form fresh martensite. Additionally, bainite forms during cooling in regions that are not fully austenitized. As depicted in Figs. 2(c)–(e), bainite distributes along ferrite grain boundaries, forming regions about 3 <sup>l</sup>m in size. Attached to the bainite are fresh martensite or islands comprising fresh martensite and retained austenite. Figs. 3(b)–(d) shows the phase distribution of AN750, AN800, and AN850. The inserted figures show the average grain size $( D _ { \mathrm { A V E } } )$ distribution of RA in each sample. [BLOCK b0058]
4.1. Multiphase microstructure mechanical response

[BLOCK b0059]
4.1.1. Model description

[BLOCK b0060]
Modeling the mechanical behavior of low-density steel with multi-level microstructures presents challenges due to the complex interactions between dislocations and interfaces, such as blocking, bypassing, or cutting mechanisms. These interactions make it difficult to describe material strength accurately using linear superposition theory.<sup>51</sup> The effectiveness of various strengthening mechanisms varies with the heterogeneous deformation of microstructure, requiring a quantitative analysis of phase composition and dislocation density.<sup>33,52</sup> The isowork theory provides a realistic and direct method for analyzing the behavior of each phase during deformation.<sup>32</sup> It assumes that tensile work is uniformly distributed across all phases,<sup>34</sup> meaning that the product of the flow stress for each phase and the corresponding true strain increment is equal:

[BLOCK b0061]
$$
\sigma_ {\mathrm{F}} \mathrm{d} \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{F}} = \sigma_ {\mathrm{B/TM}} \mathrm{d} \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{B/TM}} = \sigma_ {\mathrm{FM}} \mathrm{d} \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{FM}} = \sigma_ {\mathrm{RA}} \mathrm{d} \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{RA}} = \sigma_ {\mathrm{DIM}} \mathrm{d} \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{DIM}}\tag{1}
$$

[BLOCK b0062]
where $\sigma _ { j }$ is the rheological stress of each phase; $\mathrm { d } \varepsilon _ { j }$ is the increment of true strain for each phase. The relationship between rheological stress and true strain in the constitutive model can be expressed as the sum of the product of phase content and rheological stress. Therefore, the rheological stress depends on the composition and evolution of F, B, FM, RA, and Deformation-Induced Martensite (DIM):

[BLOCK b0063]
$$
\sigma (\varepsilon) = \sum_ {j \in \mathrm{Ph}} f _ {j} (\varepsilon_ {j}) \sigma_ {j} (\varepsilon_ {j})\tag{2}
$$

[BLOCK b0064]
where $\sigma ( \varepsilon )$ is the function of rheological stress changing with true strain; $f _ { j } ( \varepsilon _ { j } )$ is the function of phase content changing with true strain; $\sigma _ { j } ( \varepsilon _ { j } )$ is the function of rheological stress of each phase changing with true strain; Ph is the set containing each phase, $\mathrm { P h } = \{ \mathrm { F } , \mathrm { B } / \mathrm { T M }$ , FM, RA, DIM}.

4.2. Mitigating stress concentration mechanism

[BLOCK b0131]
Microscale strain localization can lead to the initiation of microcracks, while the plastic anisotropy of different microstructures can significantly alter the propagation path of cracks.<sup>81,82</sup> The necking region after tensile fracture (indicated by the red dot in Fig. 11) was selected for SEM analysis to gather key insights into the fracture behavior. Microscale voids (yellow dashed boxes) are mainly distributed at the interface of the soft domain and hard domain. If the deformation is severe enough, these voids will coalesce, leading to microvoid coalescence fracture, as depicted in Fig. 11(a). In AN750-T and AN800-T, the crack tip blunts at the hard domain interface, generating secondary cracks (yellow arrows). The driving force for crack propagation is lower than the resistance provided by the hard domains, allowing cracks to extend into other easily nucleated areas. In AN850-T, the hard domains were observed to be severed (red arrows). At 850 °??C, significant migration of carbon and manganese into the B/TM and FM regions results in local oversaturation of alloying elements, increasing the strength but also the brittleness of the hard domains. The fracture morphology shows large dimples surrounded by smaller ones (Fig. 11(b)). AN750-T displays relatively deep dimples, whereas AN800-T and AN850-T exhibit some quasi-cleavage fractures, mostly originating from fresh martensite. Dimple size distribution was analyzed using Nano Measure software (Fig. 11(c)), revealing a bimodal distribution, with AN750-T having larger average dimple sizes. This suggests that the coarse ferrite and fine hard phases effectively alleviate stress concentration during deformation.

[BLOCK b0132]
EBSD characterization was conducted on the necking region, compared to the state before tensile deformation, the RA content in AN750-T, AN800-T, and AN850-T decreased (Fig. 5(a)). The yellow dashed box areas in Fig. 12(a) were selected for magnified characterization. Stress concentration mainly occurs in the hard regions represented by B/TM and FM (highlighted in red). The white pixel areas are regions prone to micropore nucleation and expansion during deformation (Fig. 12(b)). In Fig. 12(a), characteristic lines (yellow arrows) were selected for Point-to-Point (P-T-P) orientation difference and Kikuchi pattern quality statistics (Fig. 12(c)), where $\mathrm { P - T - P _ { A v e } }$ is the average of P-T-P orientation. When the characteristic line crosses the phase boundary, the orientation difference increases sharply, and the Kikuchi pattern quality drops to a minimum, indicating significant strain and intense dislocation accumulation at the phase interface. The stress distribution behavior between the soft domain and hard domain changes significantly to accommodate the discontinuity of strain.<sup>83,84</sup>

[BLOCK b0133]
Overall, during the deformation process, the strength difference between the soft domain and hard domain leads to stress concentration within the ferrite, especially near the phase boundaries, thereby initiating crack nucleation. The continuity of the phases (continuous B/TM microstructure) can enhance the overall strength of the steels but also create pathways for crack propagation at the boundaries between the soft domain and hard domain. The crack tip, hindered by the hard domains, may induce the formation of secondary cracks, thereby mitigating stress concentration. High hardness phases (such as FM) tend to fracture through cleavage, while the soft domain may absorb energy through the dimple fracture mechanism, thus delaying the overall fracture process. Additionally, the TRIP effect significantly enhances the strain-hardening capability of the steels by absorbing energy and increasing the strength of local regions.

5. Conclusions

[BLOCK b0135]
We developed a low-density steel component with a multi-level microstructure using practical mechanical-metallurgical techniques. The research systematically analyzed the evolution of composition and dislocations within the microstructure. A constitutive model of the mechanical behavior of the multiphase structure was established, revealing the synergistic effects of various strengthening mechanisms and the multilevel toughening processes.

[BLOCK b0136]
(1) Annealing at $7 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ enabled the low-density steel with a multi-level microstructure to achieve a strength-ductility product of 36.3 GPa %, with a tensile strength of 1 068 MPa and a fracture elongation of 34.0%. At 800 ${ } ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } .$ , the tensile strength increased to 1 295 MPa, with a fracture elongation of 18.7%. These properties match those of advanced high-strength steels.

[BLOCK b0137]
(2) The constitutive model, combining the iso-work theory with the von Mises equivalent method, showed strong alignment with experimental data. It highlighted the role of back stress strengthening, consistent with changes in the bainite and tempered martensite content. Fresh martensite, with high dislocation density and carbon content, significantly enhanced strength. Deformationinduced martensite from the TRIP effect was crucial in boosting strain-hardening capacity.

[BLOCK b0138]
(3) The stress-buffering mechanism of multiphase microstructure design mainly relies on the reasonable stress/strain distribution and the soft-hard domain topology for stress transfer between phases. This effectively prevents the generation of microcracks, mitigating localized stress concentrations during deformation. The hard domains, primarily composed of bainite/tempered martensite, serve as rigid nodes for stress transfer and significantly resist crack propagation, thereby mitigating macroscopic stress concentrations.

A1. Selection of experimental materials and heat treatment temperature

[BLOCK b0147]
Weight reduction, a key strategy for energy conservation and emissions reduction, has promoted the widespread use of advanced high-strength steels in critical aerospace and low altitude flight components. Third-generation steels, such as medium-manganese and Q&P steels, typically require complex processes like quenching, tempering, or microalloying, which, despite their effectiveness, raise production costs.

[BLOCK b0148]
This study introduces a novel low-density steel that eliminates costly elements like Nb, Mo, and Ni, with a slight increase in Mn and Al content. Higher Mn content lowers the critical austenite temperatures (Ac1 and Ac3), stabilizing austenite at lower temperatures during cooling and enabling retained austenite at room temperature. It also slows the transformation to pearlite, allowing bainite or martensite to form even at slower cooling rates. Increasing the Al content can stabilize ferrite, enhance its fraction during the cooling process, and reduce the occurrence of incomplete bainite transformation, significantly accelerating the kinetics of bainite formation. Moreover, the addition of Al effectively lowers the density of the steel.

[BLOCK b0149]
The specific measurement steps for the quenching phase transformation experiment are shown in Fig. A1(a), and Fig. A1(b) illustrates the trend of sample expansion with temperature variation, where <sup>D</sup>L is the length variation of the measured sample relative to its initial reference length during heat treat ment (heating or cooling). Using the tangent method, the Ac1, Ac3, Bf, and Ms temperatures of the experimental steel were determined to be 705, 950, 380, 346 °??C, respectively, where Bf represents the bainite transformation finish temperature and Ms denotes the martensite transformation start temperature. A critical zone annealing process was applied to adjust the pro portion of ferrite and austenite, promoting the formation of a multiphase structure during cooling to achieve a multi-level microstructural strengthening effect. Aging in the lower bainite region $( 4 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ facilitates the refinement of bainitic structures, controls the kinetics of bainite transformation, prevents pearlite formation, and ensures excellent mechanical properties.
