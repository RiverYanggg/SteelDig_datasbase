A B S T R A C T

[BLOCK b0008]
In this study, the effects of annealing temperature on microstructure and mechanical properties of Fe–9Mn–5Al-0.3C (wt.%) low density steel plates via intercritical quenching-annealing process were investigated through field-emission scanning electron microscopy, electron backscatter diffraction. X-ray diffraction, and tensile tests The results showed that the experimental steel maintained micro-laminated microstructure after intercritical quenching-annealing treatment, consisting of δ-ferrite band and tempered martensite lamellar. After annealing at the range of 600–800 <sup>◦</sup>C following quenching of 1050 <sup>◦</sup>C, tempered martensite lamellar mainly changed, and affected the mechanical properties of the steel. The annealed experimental steels exhibited wide vield strength grades (460–620 MPa) with excellent ductility (40–63 %) and low yield-tensile ratio (0.65–0.75). The proportion of austenite, the density of geometrically necessary dislocations, and high-angle grain boundary density of tempered martensite region as well as the presence or absence of carbides, were considered to contributed to the mechanical properties. Austenitic mechanical ability was analyzed. Various annealed temperature led to the different levels of austenitic stability through changing the enrichment of alloyed elements in austenite, further causing different degrees of TRIP effect. TWIP effect existed during deformation according to estimation of SFE. The steel annealed at 740 <sup>◦</sup>C had maximum uniform elongation (62.4 %) and the highest product of strength and elongation (45.2 GPa%), depending on the maximum proportion of austenite transformed into martensite during deformation. However. the 680 °C annealed steel with the highest austenitic stability exhibited deficient TRIP effect, leading to the occurrence of necking. Moreover, work hardening behavior and fracture morphology of the experimental steel were analyzed.

[BLOCK b0009]
1. Introduction

[BLOCK b0010]
In the recent vears. Fe-Mn-Al–C steel aroused widespread attention in the fields of transportation metal material, offshore structure, and engineering machinery due to the social demand of lowering fuel con sumption and carbon emission. Fe–Mn–Al–C steel was a promising type of steel with low density due to Al addition and high strength [1,2]. A 1.3 % reduction in density of steel could be obtained per 1 wt% addition of Al [3]. The Al addition promoted the form of δ-ferrite, but facilitated the nucleation of short-range order structure, which was harmful for ductility of metal. Therefore, Mn, as an austenite stabilization element, was usually added to steel together with Al. According to the content of Al and Mn, low density steel was classified into four categories: ferritic steels [4,5], ferritic-based duplex steels [6,7], austenite-based duplex steels [2,8], and austenite steels [9–11]. Given that low weldability and corrosion resistance, segregation, as well as higher cost, more than 12 wt % Mn was not recommended [12–14]. Hence, medium/low-Mn duplex low density steel was a suitable choice [15].

[BLOCK b0011]
<sup>1</sup> Feifan Li and Xueliang Shang have contributed equally to this work.

[BLOCK b0012]
Dual-phase low density steel composed of δ-ferrite and martensite/ austenite was usually rolled and formed micro-laminated structure under deformation [16–19]. Below the homogenization temperature, it was difficult to refine δ-ferrite through recrystallization or phase transformation. Therefore, improving prior austenite area was an important method to enhance mechanical properties. Retained austenite could improve mechanical properties of steel by transformation induced plasticity (TRIP) and twinning induced plasticity (TWIP) [20–23]. The mechanical stability of retained austenite had been widely investigated $[ 2 4 , 2 5 ]$ . Xu et al. [26] revealed the correlation between deformation behavior and austenite stability in Mn–Al type TRIP steel. Inhomoge neous stability of retained austenite could lead to inhomogeneous and discontinuous work hardening behavior, while the heterogeneity of austenitic grain size and the orientation of retained austenite played important roles in determining the stability of austenite when defor mation. Chen et al. [27] reported that duplex lightweight steel of Fe–8Mn–6Al-0.4C exhibited excellent mechanical properties of tensile strength \~756 MPa and \~56 % elongation under synergy of TRIP and TWIP effects.

[BLOCK b0013]
The different proportions of soft phase and hard phase in Microlaminated low-density steel could lead to different mechanical proper ties. Shang et al. [28] changed the δ-ferrite content by various quenching temperature and tempering treatment to improve the mechanical properties of laminar low density steel. The total elongation of the steel was boosted from 4 % of hot-rolled state to 40 % of quenched-tempered state. Xu et al. [29] increased the retained austenitic content of micro-laminated low density steel by direction quenching and inter critical annealing, which improving the ductility form 13 %–41 % in total elongation under the assistance of TRIP effect.

[BLOCK b0014]
In this study, the heat-treatment experiments of intercritical quenching (IQ) and annealing were carried out on the steel of Fe–9Mn–5Al-0.3C. The tensile strength, 0.2 % off-set yield strength, and total elongation of the experimental steel were investigated corre sponding to the relevant laminated microstructure. The relationship between laminated microstructure and mechanical properties and the effects of the austenitic stability on deformation behavior were dis cussed. The main purpose of this research was to obtain better me chanical properties and heat-treatment parameters of the experimental steel to broaden its practical application.

3.2. Microstructure of the experimental steel under various treatments

[BLOCK b0028]
Fig. 3(a), (b) depicted SEM micrographs of the hot-rolled state (HR) of the experimental steel. A laminated microstructure consisting of δ-ferrite (δ-F) bands and lath martensite (M) was observed because of thermomechanical rolling in dual-phase region. Austenite underwent ferrite transformation and martensite transformation during the cooling process, and therefore α-ferrite (α-F) and lath martensite (M) formed. Moreover, retained austenite (RA) was observed in Martensite bands, which indicated the Mf temperature of the experimental steel was lower than room temperature.

[BLOCK b0029]
The laminated microstructure was still existing in IO-1050 due to its heredity [33], as shown in Fig. 3(c). Whereas, prior austenite grain grew up as lath martensite reversed in the holding process at $1 0 5 0 \ { } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ and prior austenite grain boundary (PAGB) moved to δ-ferrite. Accordingly, the δ-ferrite bands became thinner, and even were divided into some discontinuous δ-ferrite grains by prior austenite, as shown in Fig. 3(d). High heating temperature of $1 0 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ made the Mn content of reversed austenite become relatively low according to Fig. 2(b), decreasing the stability of austenite and increasing Ms temperature. Further, the higher cooling rate of water quenching than air cooling inhibited α-ferrite transformation and increased Ms temperature as well [33]. Therefore, martensite region of IQ-1050 was larger and retained austenite was fewer at room temperature.

[BLOCK b0030]
After annealing process, lath martensite area changed obviously. Fig. 3(e)\~(h) showed the SEM micrographs of tempered martensite in QA specimens. Carbides (Cm), which were considered to be cementite, were precipitated and widely distributed in tempered martensite, only when annealing temperature was $6 2 0 \ { } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ as shown in Fig. 3(e). Fine, reverted austenite grains were observed among the tempered martens itic laths of the four annealing temperatures. Furthermore, the new martensite from austenite by martensite transformation existed in the tempered martensite region of QA-800, as shown in the red box of Fig. 3 (h). As the annealing temperature increasing to ${ 8 0 0 } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , small austenite grains grew up, and $\mathrm { C } ,$ Mn elements in the austenite were diluted. Therefore, Ms temperature of austenite increased, resulting in Martensite transformation during air cooling.

[BLOCK b0031]
Band contrast (BC) maps with clearer austenitic distribution of the various specimens were presented at Fig. 4. The brighter and whiter regions due to high BC value were consider as ferrite, while darker regions due to low BC value were consider as lath martensite [34,35], as shown in Fig. 4(a and b). Tempered martensite in QA series possessed relative higher BC value than martensite, which was related to recovery of martensite in annealing treatment leading to decreasing of disloca tions. According to Fig. 4(a, b), δ-ferrite and prior austenite grains grew up after heating of $1 0 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ and ferrite bands were segmented by martensite lamellar, becoming dispersed. This situation was kept in annealed microstructure. For annealed specimens, fine austenite grains, including retained austenite after quenching treatment and reverted austenite, were observed to be widely distributed in tempered martensite region, as shown in Fig. $4 ( \mathbf { c } , \ \mathbf { d } , \ \mathbf { e } , \ \mathbf { f } )$ the distribution of austenite in HR was more concentrated in comparison with QA speci mens, while there was little austenite in IO-1050. as shown in Fig. 4(a and b). Although the difference of the values ob tained by the two methods existed, the changing of them with annealing temperature showed a similar trend. 3.3. Mechanical properties

[BLOCK b0033]
Fig. 5(a) and Table 3 showed engineering stress-strain curves and specific mechanical performance indicators of the experimental steels at room temperature, respectively. The hot-rolled steel showed the highest tensile strength (TS) of \~1107 MPa and poor total elongation (TE) of \~5.6 %. After intercritical quenching, the yield strength (YS) of the steel was improved, \~575 MPa, compared with HR, but TS was declined, \~929 MPa, and TE of \~2.0 % was poorest among all of specimens.

[BLOCK b0034]
Annealing process following quenching considerably enhanced total elongation of the steel. With the annealing temperature increasing, TEs of QA series steels were \~44 %, \~45.6 %, \~62.4 %, and \~50.4 %, respectively. The TEs increased as annealing temperature until anneal ing temperature was $8 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , which was relevant to martensite existing in QA-800. Moreover, all tensile specimens occurred different degrees of uniform deformation, which associated with special deformation mechanism of a large number of austenite, such as TRIP effect. Uniform elongation of all QA specimens, except QA-680, was almost equal to total elongation. For QA-680, When strain reached to around $2 5 \ \% ,$ the stress began to decrease, which meant that there was significantly necking in the tensile specimen. With the increase of annealing tem perature, both of YS and TS of QA specimens were gradually decreased. It was worth noting that the YS and TS of QA-620 was much higher than that of other QA specimens, which was principally associated with precipitated carbides in QA-620. The products of tensile strength and total elongation (PSE) of all QA specimens were more than 30 GPa%, showing superior comprehensive mechanical properties. PSE of QA-740, \~45.2 GPa%, was the highest one, which means that QA-740 owns the most excellent combination of strength and ductility. Furthermore, all QA specimens exhibited low yield-to-tensile ratio. Overall, the experi mental steel after QA treatment exhibited wide yield strength grades (460–620 MPa) under the conditions of excellent ductility and low yieldto-tensile ratio, and was a typical third generation advanced high strength steel. Relying on the advantage of low density, the experi mental steel could be used for high strength automobile steel plates, substituted for TRIP 590T/400Y from SAE J2745-2007 standard [36]

3.4. Fracture characterization

[BLOCK b0041]
As shown in Fig. 6 was SEM morphology of tensile fracture of QA specimens, which all exhibited mixed fracture feature with cleavage in the red dashed areas, quasi-cleavage in the blue dashed areas, and dimples in the yellow dashed areas. Second cracks were observed in these fractures as well due to stress concentration in interface between δ and γ/α. For QA-680 and QA-740 shown in Fig. 6(b and c), cleavage/quasi-cleavage and dimples areas have not sensible borders, and instead crisscrossed together. Furthermore, larger dimples pointed by yellow arrows existed in the fractures than normal agglomerated dimples in the yellow dashed areas. In Fig. 6(d) of QA-800, cleavage layer showed a mainly feature of cleavage step, which alternated with dimples layer.

4. Discussion

[BLOCK b0046]
4.1. Microstructure evolution and mechanical properties

[BLOCK b0047]
Mechanical properties of steels are intimately correlated with the microstructure. Adding Al and Mn elements promoted the formation of a dual-phase layered microstructure in the steel used in this research, consisting of δ-ferrite and austenite. Upon cooling to room temperature, the austenite, due to its weak stability, underwent martensitic trans formation. The variations in prior austenite lamellar in various treat ments exerted important influence on mechanical properties of the present steels. Fig. 7 showed band contrast maps, focusing on prior austenite lamellar, of the present steels under various processes, and corresponding kernel average misorientation (KAM) maps and grain boundary distribution maps. geometrically necessary dislocations (GND) densities of corresponding specimens were obtained by following equation [41,42]:

Annealing process following IQ process considerably improved the comprehensive mechanical properties of the steel in this study, but decreased the yield strength of QA specimens. On the one hand, during annealing processing, retained austenite fraction after quenching increased, while reverted austenite nucleated and grew up at martensite lath gaps, with the diffusion of the carbon and manganese into austenite grains, and aluminum into martensite and ferrite. It was widely accepted that ductile deformation started in soft phase universally, such as ferrite, austenite [45]. With increase of annealing temperature, the fraction of austenite gradually increased until QA-800 (Table 2). That the soft phase of austenite increasing will be prone to induce ductile deformation, leading to yield strength decreasing. On the other hand, recovery effect of annealing treatment led to the decrease of the GND density in tempered martensite and austenite. With the increase of annealing temperature, the martensitic and austenitic GND values of annealed specimens decreased continuously. The decrease of the GND density will be easier to cause dislocation slip during deformation, decreasing the yield strength of tempered martensite lamellar. Low GND density in tempered martensite promoted the coordinate deformation between tempered martensite and δ-ferrite band, which was beneficial to plas ticity of the steel [28].

[BLOCK b0058]
Therefore, the fraction of austenite, and the GND density of tempered martensite lamellar are the mainly reasons of decreasing yield strength of the experimental steel by annealing process. Furthermore, for QA-620, the precipitation of carbides strongly retarded dislocation gliding to greatly enhance yield and tensile strength, while the decrease of alloy elements content in matrix reduced the lattice distortion of tempered martensite to rise the deformability of the steel, which was positive to the ductility. For QA-800, though its austenite fraction was slightly lower than that of QA-740, its GND densities of tempered martensite and austenite was lower, still leading to lower YS.

[BLOCK b0059]
Grain boundaries of alloy materials plays a significantly undeniable role in mechanical properties. Depending on the angle of boundaries, grain boundaries are generally classified into three ranges: 5°-15°. 15°. 45<sup>◦</sup>, and ${ > } 4 5 ^ { \circ }$ [28,46], where high angle boundaries (>15<sup>◦</sup>) are regar ded as critical to affect mechanical properties [47]. A Python machine learning model developed by Li et al. [48] based on crystallographic information were used to correctly identify the various boundary types and exactly calculate the boundary density, as shown in Fig. 7(a-2,b-2, $\mathtt { c - 2 , d - 2 , e - 2 , f - 2 ) }$ Given that the coarse δ-ferrite grains, the grain boundaries mainly gathered in martensite layer. Hence, grain bound aries can affect the mechanical properties of the present steel only by affecting the martensitic properties. As is seen in Fig. 8(b), austenitic grain (AG) boundaries (15<sup>◦</sup>-45<sup>◦</sup>), including austenitic boundaries and prior austenitic boundaries, and block boundaries $( > 4 5 ^ { \circ } )$ in all speci mens were the highest two of boundary density. After quenching of $1 0 5 0 \ { } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ the density of block boundary was higher than that of AG boundary, and the situation of HR was opposite, which indicated that more austenitic fraction underwent martensitic transformation than that of HR. the density of block boundary increased also contributed to the yield strength of IQ-1050. For QA specimens, with the increase of annealed temperature, AG boundary density increased. When annealed temperature being 800 <sup>◦</sup>C, the density instead decreased due to some coarse austenite grains. Meanwhile, block boundary density decreased continuously with annealed temperature. With the increase of annealed temperature, increasingly reverted austenite nucleated and grew up at the martensitic lath gaps, which resulting in that block boundaries gradually replaced by AG boundaries. Obviously, the variation trend of AG boundary density was consistent with that of austenitic volume fraction, but it did not play an effectively role in enhancing strength. Luo et al. [49] had reported that block boundaries remarkably contributed to yield strength of martensitic stainless steel. It was considered that the decrease of block boundary with angle of ${ > } 4 5 ^ { \circ }$ in density, to some extent, contributed to the decrease of yield strength of the annealed specimens, with the increase of annealed temperature [50].

[BLOCK b0072]
4.3. Deformation behavior

[BLOCK b0073]
The complete process of deformation was analyzed here. Fig. 10 depicted separately the work hardening rate (WHR) curves and true stress-strain curves of the QA specimens. The WHR curves of all QA specimens had an analogous shape, and could be divided into three stages based on the slopes of WHR curves. In stage I, dislocation gliding started sequentially in ferrite and austenite, as well as tempered martensite, according to the difference of phase in hardness [29]. Particularly, after heating process of $1 0 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and annealed treatment, δ-ferrite grain apparently grew up, while small grains were gradually engulfed by larger grains. Hence, the grain boundary density of ferrite apparently decreased, while dislocation mean free path increased. Derived from large dislocation mean free path of ferrite, the WHR sharply decreased [57,58]. In stage II, the decline of the WHR obviously slowed down, which attributed to several hardening mechanisms. The hindrance of grain boundaries and high-density dislocation tangles to dislocation movement, as well as TRIP effect, could enhance WHR. Meanwhile, dislocation gliding played a mainly role in reducing WHR. Moreover, the finite dislocation annihilation at grain boundaries [59], as well as cross slip among dislocations [60], were also expected to contribute to lowering WHR. Apparently, dislocation gliding was still dominant as WHR slowly decreased in stage II.

[BLOCK b0074]
In stage III, the WHR instead increased slowly, which meant TRIP effect dominating. Due to finer austenite grains after annealing process, the effect of austenitic grain size on TRIP effect was non-negligible. Fig. 11 showed austenitic grain size statistics of QA specimens before deformation. The distributions of austenite grain size in all QA speci mens had a similar trend. With the decrease of grain size, the quantities of austenite grain gradually increased, and the size of around 90 % of the grains was less than 4.8 μm. The finer austenite grain possessed higher enrichment degree of alloy elements, and thus corresponding austenitic stability was higher [61]. The fine austenite grains possessing different stability transformed into martensite only when a certain critical strain was attained [62]. Therefore, in stage $\mathrm { I I } ,$ a small number of austenite grains with relatively large size underwent TRIP effect, producing weak hardening effect on WHR. As true strain increasing during stage III, a larger number of small austenite grains transformed into martensite, producing stronger hardening effect to make WHR enduringly increasing. The martensite produced during the strain process not only acted as an obstacle of dislocation gliding, but also as a site of dislocation accumulation and a source of partial dislocations [7]. Further, the martensite due to fine austenite grains, as small particles of hard phase, distributed widely in tempered martensite area, which could hinder dislocation gliding more strongly, boosting WHR. This was similar to second-phase particles strengthening mechanism. Moreover, based on the estimation of SFE (section 4.2), TWIP effect could occur when deformation. TWIP effect might play a role in the later stage of stage III [27].

During tensile tests, ductility deformation led to dislocation accu mulation at the interfaces of δ-ferrite band and tempered martensite lamellar. Due to the difference in hardness between the two phases [29], stress concentration was prone to occur at their interfaces, to induce crack initiation. Once the crack forms, it would be captured by δ-ferrite, and propagated into and along δ-ferrite band, leading to the formation of second cracks like deep valleys (Fig. 6) [39,63]. Moreover, the crack propagating into δ-ferrite could be blunted due to the soft phase of δ-ferrite, which produced high resistance to the cracking [63]. Strain-induced martensitic transformation led to volume expansion, promoting the closure of cracks [64], which was positive to the ductility. Deficient high-angle grain boundaries of coarse δ-ferrite cannot effec tively hinder microcrack propagation. Hence, cleavage morphology formed in δ-ferrite as microcrack propagating (Fig. 6). This was because {100} cleavage was a natural failure mode of many BCC crystals, including iron [65].

5. Conclusion

[BLOCK b0080]
In the present study, the effect of annealing temperature on micro structure and mechanical properties of the low-density δ-TRIP steel of Fe-8.9Mn-4.6Al-0.29C via intercritical quenching-annealing was inves tigated. The austenitic stability and deformation behavior of the presented steel was analyzed. Based on current experimental results, the main conclusions were as followed:

[BLOCK b0081]
(1) The laminated hot-rolled experimental steel after intercritical quenching and annealing still maintained the micro-laminated microstructure, consisting of δ-ferrite band and tempered martensite lamellar. After annealing, a large number of fine austenite grains widely distributed in tempered martensite. With annealing temperature increasing, the proportion of austenite at room temperature increased. However, after annealing $\mathrm { a t 8 0 0 } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ some austenite underwent martensitic transformation during air cooling, which instead led to a decrease of austenite. $\mathrm { A t } 6 2 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ of annealing temperature, carbides precipitated in tempered martensite region.

[BLOCK b0082]
(2) The experimental steel after annealing treatment exhibited wide yield strength grades (460–620 MPa) under the conditions of extremely excellent ductility and low yield-to-tensile ratio, could substituted for TRIP 580T/400Y high strength automobile plates. Specifically, the $7 4 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ annealed steel had the highest product of strength and elongation, up $\mathbf { t o } \sim 4 5 . 2 \ \mathrm { G P a } \% ,$ and the maximum uniform elongation of \~62.4 %. The yield and tensile strength of annealed specimens decreased as annealing temperature, which attributed to the factors of the proportion of austenite, geomet rically necessary dislocations density, and block boundary den sity of tempered martensite.

[BLOCK b0083]
(3) The partitioning of C and Mn elements significantly affected the mechanical stability of austenite. Specifically, austenite in the specimen annealed at 680 <sup>◦</sup>C possessed the highest stability. With annealing temperature increasing from $6 8 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , austenitic stabil ity decreased due to the dilution of austenitic alloy elements. When annealed at $6 2 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ precipitated carbides in specimens led to the decrease of alloy elements in matrix, resulting in lower austenitic stability than that of 680 <sup>◦</sup>C annealed specimens. Various austenitic stability contributed to various levels of TRIP effect during deformation.

[BLOCK b0084]
(4) All annealed specimens exhibited three-stage work hardening characteristics, in which stage II and III resulted from the competition between dislocation gliding and TRIP effect, com bined with the assistance of TWIP effect according to SFEs. The specimen annealed only at 680 <sup>◦</sup>C exhibited apparently necking due to deficient TRIP effect, and afterward underwent a second stage of uniform elongation until failure.
