A B S T R A C T

[BLOCK b0009]
A novel low alloy ultrahigh strength steel was developed by introducing extremely high-density NiAl clusters (\~ 4.88 × 10<sup>24</sup> m<sup>−</sup> <sup>3</sup>). The steel exhibited an ultimate tensile strength of \~ 1.9 GPa, a total elongation of \~ 15 %, a Unotch impact absorption energy of \~ 80 J and a fracture toughness of 108 MPa⋅m<sup>1/2</sup>. The precipitation behavior and the toughening mechanisms were systematically investigated. Highly dispersed nano-NiAl clusters and submicron ε-carbides precipitate independently, although a small fraction of NiAl clusters interacts with Si during precipitation, thereby increasing the availability of heterogeneous nucleation sites. NiAl clusters promote uniform dislocation distribution, suppressing pile-ups at boundaries and delaying crack initiation. The study revealed multi-scale toughening mechanisms, including large dimple growth, uniform plastic deformation, secondary crack formation, and extensive crack deflection, which collectively result in a \~ 23 % improvement in impact absorption energy. These findings demonstrate that dual-precipitation of NiAl cluster and ε-carbides and dislocation-precipitate interactions provides an effective strategy for achieving high toughness at a tensile strength of \~ 1.9 GPa in low alloy ultrahigh strength steels.

[BLOCK b0010]
1. Introduction

[BLOCK b0011]
Low alloy ultrahigh strength steels, such as 300 M, D6AC, and Eglin, are commonly used in aircraft landing gear, rocket casings, and heavyduty equipment owing to their excellent strength-toughness combina tion and cost efficiency [1–3]. Upon quenching and tempering, these steels achieve tensile strengths exceeding 1.8 GPa through a combina tion of grain refinement by MC carbides, dislocation strengthening within the martensitic matrix, and further enhancement by the precip itation of submicron-sized ε-carbides during low-temperature tempering [4–6].

[BLOCK b0012]
300 M is among the highest-strength low alloy steels currently available. Mandal et al. [6] reported that 300 M tempered at 850 <sup>◦</sup>C followed by water quenching achieved a tensile strength of 1991 MPa, but exhibited a limited V-notch impact absorption energy of only 27.6 J. With increasing tensile strength, a reduction in impact toughness is commonly observed, reflecting the inherent strength-toughness tradeoff that continues to constrain the structural reliability of such steels

[BLOCK b0013]
[7–9].

[BLOCK b0014]
In efforts to enhance the toughness of low-alloy steels, considerable attention has been directed toward microstructural optimization by controlling high-angle boundaries [10] and tailoring the amount and distribution of retained austenite [5,11].However, these approaches typically rely on deformation or cryogenic treatments, which involve complex procedures and offer limited process control. In contrast, the introduction of nanoscale precipitates has emerged as a more favorable strategy in recent years, offering a simultaneous improvement in strength and toughness. Although conventional carbides such as M₂C [12] and MC [13] can provide effective strengthening, their formation depends on C, which may partially degrade the matrix. To overcome this limitation, increasing attention has been directed toward intermetallic compounds-particularly nanoscale NiAl precipitates-which are capable of delivering substantial strengthening [14,15] under medium-lowcarbon conditions while maintaining good ductility making them one of the most promising directions in current alloy design [16,17].

[BLOCK b0015]
B2-NiAl shows exceptional potential in high-alloy maraging steels due to its high lattice coherency with the α-Fe matrix (lattice misfit <3 %) [18], metastable nanocluster features, and dislocation shearing mechanisms. Peng et al. [19] demonstrated that the introduction of Mo rich clusters could effectively promote the precipitation of high-density coherent NiAl phases. Benefiting from low lattice mismatch and pro nounced ordering effects, the resulting maraging steel exhibited a tensile strength of 2099 MPa along with a fracture toughness exceeding 80 $\mathrm { { M P a } } \mathrm { { \cdot m } } ^ { 1 / 2 }$ . This study also offers new insights into the strengthtoughness design of low-alloy steels, suggesting that the introduction of nanoscale NiAl precipitates with high number density may enhance toughness while maintaining a tensile strength near 1900 MPa.

[BLOCK b0016]
Accordingly, a low alloy ultrahigh strength steel was designed with dual precipitation of ε-carbides and NiAl clusters with an extremely high number density. This study systematically investigates the nucleation mechanisms of NiAl and ε phases, evaluates the influence of NiAl clus ters on the hierarchical martensitic microstructure, and elucidates their precipitation behavior and toughening contributions.

2. Experimental

[BLOCK b0018]
2.1. Materials, treatment, and mechanical properties

[BLOCK b0019]
Table 1 presents the chemical compositions of the two experimental steels, referred to as 0Al and 1.3Al for simplicity. To ensure sufficient NiAl content in the 1.3Al steel, the Ni and Al concentrations were appropriately increased based on the composition of the 0Al steel. Both materials were produced by vacuum induction melting followed by vacuum arc remelting (VIM + VAR) to obtain 500 kg ingots, which were subsequently forged into billets with Φ170 mm. Specimens for micro structural and mechanical characterization were extracted from the quarter-radius position.

[BLOCK b0020]
The two steels underwent solution treatment at 970 <sup>◦</sup>C for 1 h to dissolve carbides, followed by tempering at $1 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 2 h, with water quenching after each step. The samples of 0Al steel before and after tempering are denoted as 0Al,WQ and 0Al,2 h, respectively, while those of 1.3Al steel are designated as 1.3Al,WQ and 1.3Al,2 h. Room temperature tensile tests were conducted on specimens with a diameter of 5 mm and a gauge length of 25 mm using an testing machine (Instron 5985) at a strain rate of $0 . 0 0 1 \ s ^ { - 1 }$ . Charpy U-notch impact toughness $( K U _ { 2 } )$ was measured on specimens with dimensions of 10 × $1 0 \times 5 5 ~ \mathrm { m m } ^ { 3 }$ using an impact tester (Instron 750MPX). Both the spec imen surfaces and notches were mechanically polished to minimize surface roughness effects. Three tests per sample condition were per formed. Plane strain fracture toughness tests were conducted on a servo hydraulic fatigue machine (Instron 8801) using a three-point bending configuration. Specimens with a thickness of $^ { 2 5 }$ mm and a width of 50 mm were machined, with a 3 mm fatigue pre-crack introduced at the notch tip. During testing, a loading rate of 38 KN/min was applied. The conditional fracture toughness value $K _ { \mathrm { Q } }$ was calculated in accordance with ASTM E399 using the following equation:

[BLOCK b0021]
$$
K _ {\mathrm{Q}} = \left(\frac {F _ {\mathrm{Q}} S}{B W ^ {3 / 2}}\right) \times \mathrm{f} \left(\frac {a}{W}\right)\tag{1}
$$

[BLOCK b0022]
where $F _ { Q }$ is the conditional value of the load; $S , a , B ,$ W are the loading span, crack length, thickness and width of the bend specimens, respec tively; and f is the dimensional factor which depends on the ratio of a to W.

2.2. Microstructural characterization and data analysis

[BLOCK b0026]
The microstructures of the specimens were characterized by scan ning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Sigma 300) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-F200). SEM specimens were cut from impact specimens, ground, polished, and subsequently etched with a 4 vol% nitric acid-ethanol solution. Thin foils for TEM were extracted from the matrix, ground to 50 μm, punched into Φ3 mm discs, and electropolished by double-jet thinning at 30 V and $- 2 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ using a 10 vol % perchloric acid alcohol solution. TEM imaging was conducted at an accelerating voltage of 200 KV. Regions near fracture surfaces were sampled by focused ion beam (FIB) milling.

[BLOCK b0027]
Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) was conducted on specimens measuring $1 0 \times 1 0 \times 5 ~ \mathrm { m m } ^ { 3 }$ , which were mechanically polished with 0.02 μm colloidal silica suspension to eliminate surface residual stresses. EBSD data were collected using a Bruker e-Flash FS detector mounted on the SEM, operating at an accelerating voltage of 20 KV and a step size of 0.1 μm. Subsequent data processing was performed using Aztec Crystal v2.1 software.

[BLOCK b0028]
Phase analysis was conducted using X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku D/Max-2550) with a Co radiation source operated at 40 KV and 150 mA. Scans were performed from 20<sup>◦</sup> to 110<sup>◦</sup> at a scanning rate of 2<sup>◦</sup>/min. The volume fraction of retained austenite was determined by the direct comparison method, assuming negligible diffraction contributions from carbides. The dislocation density (ρ) was estimated using the Williamson-Hall method [20]:

[BLOCK b0029]
$$
\frac {\Delta 2 \theta \mathrm{cos} \theta}{\lambda} = \frac {0 . 9}{D} + \frac {2 \varepsilon \mathrm{sin} \theta}{\lambda}\tag{2}
$$

[BLOCK b0030]
where λ is the X-ray wavelength (0.154 nm), D is the apparent crystallite size, and ε represents the lattice strain. The dislocation density (ρ) was calculated based on the following equation $\begin{array} { r } { [ 2 1 ] [ : \rho = \frac { 2 \sqrt { 3 \varepsilon } } { D b } ; } \end{array}$ , where b de notes the magnitude of the Burgers vector.

[BLOCK b0031]
Atom probe tomography (APT, LEAP 5000XR) was performed in voltage-pulsing mode. Specimens were prepared by electrochemical polishing of needle-shaped blanks and subsequently analyzed at a base temperature of 50 K under a pulse fraction of 0.2 and a repetition rate of 125 KHZ. The detection rate was controlled at 5 ions per 1 k pulses by gradually increasing the applied voltage. APT data were reconstructed and analyzed using CAMECA IVAS 3.6.8 software, and the average diameter and number density of precipitates were evaluated [22].

4. Discussion

[BLOCK b0068]
4.1. Precipitation behavior

[BLOCK b0069]
APT and TEM analyses (Figs 3, 5 and 6) reveal that, in contrast to the 0Al steel, the 1.3Al steel contains not only ε-carbides formed during low temperature tempering at ${ 1 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } C } ,$ but also uniformly dispersed NiAl clusters with an extremely high number density of $4 . 8 8 \times 1 0 ^ { - 2 4 } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 3 }$ . The hcp-structured ε-carbides measure approximately 100–500 nm in length and 2–4 nm in thickness, and are regularly aligned along the martensitic laths. In contrast, B2-ordered NiAl clusters with an average radius of approximately 0.57 nm are uniformly dispersed throughout the matrix.

[BLOCK b0070]
APT isoconcentration maps (Fig. 5(c) and Fig. 6A) reveal that the central region of the ε-carbides contains a carbon concentration as high as 8.91 at.%, while the concentrations of Ni and Al are \~ 5.50 at.% and \~ 2,60 at.%. respectively. As shown in Fig. 5(c). the Ni and Al contents remain essentially constant in this region. In contrast, the region sur rounding NiAl clusters consistently maintains a carbon concentration of \~ 1.0 at.% (Fig. 6B and C). These findings indicate that ε-carbides and NiAl clusters nucleate and grow independently during tempering, with no observable elemental overlap or competitive partitioning between C and Ni/Al.

[BLOCK b0071]
The lattice parameter of NiAl (\~2.882 nm) closely matches that of the martensitic matrix (\~ 2.866 nm), resulting in a lattice mismatch of only \~ 0.5 % [18,33]. The presence of Mo and Cr atoms expands the matrix lattice, thereby further reducing the lattice mismatch and lowering the nucleation barrier [34]. which promotes the extremely uniform precipitation of NiAl clusters.

[BLOCK b0072]
Fig. 6C reveals that, while most NiAl clusters are uniformly distrib uted within the martensitic matrix, a fraction preferentially precipitates along Si- and Cr-enriched banded zones. In these localized regions, the Si content rises significantly from 4.21 at.% in the matrix to 11.21 at.% indicating solute segregation. The NiAl clusters in this region exhibit slightly larger sizes than the matrix-dispersed counterparts, while still retaining nanoscale dimensions. Previous studies have reported that NiAl tends to nucleate at defects such as dislocations [29] and bound aries [19,35]. However, the absence of carbon segregation in the banded region observed in Fig. 6C suggests that it does not correspond to a typical phase boundary, but is more likely associated with nondislocation intragranular defects [29,36]. In conventional low alloy steels, Si has been reported to stabilize the ε phase and delay its trans formation to cementite during tempering at elevated temperatures $( 2 5 0 { - } 3 5 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ , thereby mitigating temper embrittlement [37]. However, under lower tempering conditions $( < 2 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ , no significant variation in Si content is typically observed, nor is it known to induce other reactions [38]. However, in the present study, Si enrichment was observed around NiAl clusters after tempering at $1 8 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } \ \left( \mathrm { F i g } . \ 6 \mathbf { C } \right)$ , indicating an inter action between Si and NiAl. These Si-enriched regions are considered to provide favorable nucleation sites for NiAl clusters, thereby facilitating their precipitation.

4.2. Dislocation regulation by NiAl cluster

[BLOCK b0077]
To investigate the interaction between NiAl clusters and dislocations, statistical analysis was conducted on multiple fields near the tensile fracture surface (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Fig. S3). Under sustained stress, a large number of dislocation cells are formed within the martensitic laths. Compared with the reference steel, the dislocation distribution in the 1.3Al steel appears more homogeneous.

[BLOCK b0078]
Despite being sheared by mobile dislocations during deformation [39], the ultrafine and densely distributed NiAl clusters significantly hinder dislocation motion by increasing cluster density and reducing the dislocations mean free path, thereby enhancing lattice friction and promoting a more uniform dislocation structure [40]. The distribution of KAM values reflected the dislocation density of the specimen surface [41]. Fig. 9 demonstrates that the uniform KAM distribution in the 1.3Al steel facilitates effective stress transfer to the interior of the matrix during fracture, enabling coordinated deformation and mitigating stress concentration near the main crack. The KAM value in the matrix of 1.3Al and 2 h steels is low (0.79, as shown in Fig. S5), but near the fracture surface, it increases to 1.31, indicating that the elevated KAM values are not intrinsic to the alloy, but rather a result of stress dispersion near the crack. This confirms that NiAl clusters facilitate the formation of spatially uniform dislocation structures through cooperative in teractions with dislocations. This suppresses entanglement and precipitate-induced pile-ups, mitigating local stress concentrations [42]. While the 1.3Al steel exhibits a slightly higher dislocation density of ${ \sim } 2 . 2 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 5 } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ compared to ${ \sim } 2 . 0 1 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 5 } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ in the 0Al steel, its improved dislocation homogeneity contributes to enhanced fracture toughness.

4.3. Toughening mechanisms

[BLOCK b0080]
Toughening mechanisms are typically governed by the synergistic effect of microstructural features in regulating stress distribution and accommodating plastic deformation during crack initiation and propa gation [43]. Based on the result of $\mathrm { F i g . } 7 \mathrm { - F i g . } 1 0        $ , the toughening mechanisms in 1.3Al steel are analyzed in three aspects:

[BLOCK b0081]
Prior to $P _ { \mathrm { M } } ,$ both the 0Al and 1.3Al steels exhibited significant plastic deformation, with strain hardening raising $P _ { \mathrm { M } } \tan \mathrm { \sim } 4 0 \mathrm { K N } ( \mathrm { F i g . 7 } )$ . Stable crack propagation followed, with the 1.3Al steel absorbing more impact energy, consistent with fractographic evidence (Fig. 8). Fracture in the 1.3Al steel was dominated by ductile mechanisms involving the growth and coalescence of large and small dimples. Under applied stress, numerous voids are generated within the matrix, which expand and coalesce into larger dimples. This void formation and growth dissipate a substantial portion of the impact energy during fracture [44].

[BLOCK b0082]
Secondly, the higher E observed in the 1.3Al steel (Fig. 7) reflects enhanced plastic deformability and crack resistance. The uniformly distributed dislocation network effectively constrains the formation of pile-ups during the interaction of dislocations, thereby suppressing localized stress intensification [35]. During fracture, the shearable and coherent nature of NiAl clusters facilitates progressive strain delocal ization into the surrounding matrix, enabling extensive and coordinated plastic deformation. This redistribution of strain not only elevates the energy threshold required for crack initiation, but also delays the onset of critical stress concentrations, thereby enhancing the material’s ability to sustain higher applied loads without premature failure [35].

[BLOCK b0083]
Additionally, Dense packet boundaries and dislocations (low-angle boundaries) act as barriers to dislocation motion [27].As shown in Fig. 10, the 1.3Al steel exhibits frequent crack deflections and voids with a length of \~10 μm near the fracture surface due to localized strain accumulation [45]. The coalescence of voids can initiate secondary cracks within the matrix, leading to deflection of the main crack and enhanced crack tip blunting $[ 4 6 , 4 7 ]$ . This crack path deviation increases the propagation distance and promotes greater energy dissipation [48]. Such a toughening effect is particularly evident in the 1.3Al steel, where $E _ { \mathrm { p } }$ exceeds that of the 0Al counterpart by 4.6 J (Fig. 7), confirming the effectiveness of this mechanism. The less pronounced increase in $E _ { \mathrm { p } }$ relative to $E _ { \mathrm { i } }$ provides additional evidence that the improvement in toughness is predominantly governed by the structural uniformity imparted by the dispersion of NiAl clusters, rather than by the 0.8 % increase in retained austenite content $[ 5 , 4 9 , 5 0 ]$

[BLOCK b0084]
Fig. 11 schematically depicts the crack propagation mechanisms operative during impact fracture in both low alloy ultrahigh strength steels. In the 1.3Al steel, uniformly distributed NiAl clusters and ε-car bides interact with dislocations in distinct ways: ε-carbides promote dislocation cell formation through bypassing [51], while closely spaced NiAl clusters are directly sheared, promoting a more uniform spatial distribution. This refined dislocation network suppresses localized strain accumulation and raises the energy barrier for crack initiation. At high stress levels, the crack path is further deflected by NiAl-induced per turbations and disrupted by secondary cracks initiated via voids coa lescence. These combined effects increase crack tortuosity and retard propagation, resulting in greater energy absorption. Collectively, the hierarchical dislocation-obstacle architecture enables a favorable strength-toughness synergy by dispersing strain, delaying failure, and impeding crack advancement.

5. Conclusions

[BLOCK b0086]
A novel low alloy ultrahigh strength steel was developed by intro ducing coherent NiAl clusters, achieving superior strength-toughness synergy. The main findings are:

[BLOCK b0087]
1. High-density coherent NiAl clusters $( \sim 4 . 8 8 \times 1 0 ^ { 2 4 } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 3 } )$ with high nucleation rates and uniform dispersion were successfully introduced. A small fraction of NiAl clusters exhibits a specific interaction with Si, which increases the availability of heterogeneous nucleation sites.

[BLOCK b0088]
2. NiAl clusters optimized dislocation distributions, thereby enhancing strain hardening, plastic deformation capacity, and delaying crack initiation.

[BLOCK b0089]
3. Toughening mechanisms including large dimple growth, extensive plastic deformation, secondary crack formation via voids coales cence, and frequent crack deflection significantly enhanced impact energy absorption.

[BLOCK b0090]
4. The NiAl-cluster-strengthened steel exhibited a YS of $1 2 7 2 \pm 5 2$ MPa, a UTS of $1 8 7 6 \pm 2 5 \mathrm { M P a } ,$ , a TE of $1 3 . 7 \pm 0 . 4 \% , \mathrm { a } K U _ { 2 }$ of 80 ± 3 J, and a K of $1 0 8 \mathrm { \ M P a } { \cdot } \mathrm { m } ^ { 1 / 2 } ;$ , demonstrating excellent strengthtoughness balance.

[BLOCK b0091]
Overall, the uniform precipitation of NiAl clusters offers an effective pathway for enhancing the strength-toughness synergy of low alloy martensitic steels, providing insights for the design of next-generation ultrahigh strength materials.
