A B S T R A C T

[BLOCK b0008]
The microstructure and mechanical properties of Fe-35Mn-xAl-0.1C (x = 4, 6, 8, 10) low-density steels were studied using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and tensile machine. It is found that the microstructure evolves with the increasing of Al content from a single austenite phase steel for 4 %Al, to a ferrite-based duplex steel for 8 %Al, but nearly a fully ferrite phase steel with 10 %Al. As the Al content is increased from 4 wt % to 8 wt %, the yield strength and tensile strength are significantly increased from 239 MPa to 552 MPa–455 MPa and 740 MPa, respectively, while the uniform elongation is only slightly decreased from 38.5 % to 31.0 %. However, as the Al content is increased up to 10 %, the yield strength is gradually increased to 585 MPa, but the tensile elongation is sharply decreased down to about 4.6 %. Based on the thorough microstructure examination of the dislocation type and dislocation pattern and the microstructure evolution during tensile deformation, it is concluded that the excellent combination of the mechanical property of the 8Al steel is realized by the activation of the edge-typed dislocation and dynamic slip band refinement in the ferrite grain mitigated by the surrounded austenite grains, which enhanced the strain hardening rate significantly. This study not only reveals a new ductility-enhancing mechanism of ferrite + austenite duplex steel, but also provides a new route for designing BCC based low-density steel with high strength and high ductility.

[BLOCK b0009]
1. Introduction

[BLOCK b0010]
As one of the new generation materials, the lightweight steel has been paid much more attention over the past two decades due to its excellent combination of mechanical properties and low density [1–6]. The lightweight steels mainly include austenitic low-density steel with high carbon, high manganese, and high aluminum contents, the duplex low-density steel with relatively low contents of carbon, manganese, and aluminum, and the ferritic low-density steel with the lowest contents of carbon, manganese, and aluminum [2]. Al-alloyed low-density steels exhibit an attractive combination of yield strength, tensile strength, elongation and work-hardening capacity [2,7–9]. The excellent me chanical properties of high stacking fault energy (SFE) austenitic low-density steel are attributed to the planar glide of dislocations and the dynamic slip band refinement (DSBR) mechanism [10–13]. The planar glide of dislocations in high stacking fault energy austenite originates from the “slip plane softening” effect caused by dislocation

[BLOCK b0011]
shearing of ordered domains [12].

[BLOCK b0012]
In ferritic low-density steel, plastic instability exists and is induced by the introduction of B2 and DO ordered domains [14–16]. It was found that the dislocations gradually transitioned from single mixed dislocations to paired screw dislocations with the increase of Al content in Fe-Al ferritic low-density steel [14,16], which was associated with low-energy screw dislocations [14] and high Peierls-Nabarro stresses [16]. In traditional fully body-centered cubic (BCC) alloys, the gliding velocities of edge dislocations are typically much higher than those of screw dislocations due to the low Peierls-Nabarro stresses [17–19]. As a result, edge dislocation segments are readily annihilated during defor mation, and screw dislocations survive in the deformed microstructure [20,21]. However, edge dislocations are observed in multi-component BCC phases, such as titanium alloys [22] and high-entropy alloys [20, 21]. In these alloys, the significant increase of Peierls-Nabarro stress for edge dislocation glide, caused by substantial local atomic volume mismatch or ordered domains, may effectively impede dislocation motion and consequently promote extensive dislocation multiplication [20]. However, edge dislocations in ordered ferrite have not been re ported to date in ferritic matrix lightweight steels.

[BLOCK b0013]
The research on duplex low-density steels primarily focused on austenite-based duplex low-density steels [23–25]. The introduction of B2 and $\mathrm { D O } _ { 3 }$ ordered phases into high-aluminum ferrite can bring a significant enhancement in yield strength, but simultaneously lead to an increase in brittleness [15,23,24,26,27]. It was found that B2 and DO3 ordered domains appear in Fe-Al alloys during the cooling process when the Al content exceeds 6.2 % [28,29]. In the Fe21Mn10Al1C5Ni light weight steel, dislocations within the B2 particles exhibit planar glide characteristics, which may be attributed to the dislocation shearing of $\mathrm { D O } _ { 3 }$ nanoparticles [24]. Furthermore, planar slip of dislocations asso ciated with the shearing of ordered structures has also been observed in titanium alloys [30,31] and high-entropy alloys [32]. Therefore, the dislocation deformation characteristics of ferrite will be significantly influenced by the interactions between dislocations and ordered phases. However, the systematic work in this aspect has not yet been conducted to investigate the work hardening mechanisms of high-aluminum ferritic matrix-based lightweight steels.

[BLOCK b0014]
To address this issue of both dislocation patterns and work hardening behaviors, Fe35MnxAl0.1C (x = 4, 6, 8, 10 wt%) low-density steels were designed. Among them, Fe35Mn4Al0.1C is an austenitic lightweight steel, Fe35Mn6Al0.1C is an austenite based duplex lightweight steel, and Fe35Mn8Al0.1C and Fe35Mn10Al0.1C are ferrite based duplex lightweight steels. Tensile deformations were carried out to reveal the microstructural effects on the work hardening behaviors and mechanical properties of the designed steels. And both scanning electron microscope (SEM) and transmission electron microscope (TEM) were applied to reveal the microstructure evolution in terms of dislocation patterns of the designed steel during deformation process. The effects of micro structure on the dislocation type, dislocation pattern, work hardening behavior and mechanical property will be discussed, which is expected to give a thorough understanding on the evolution of dislocation pattern and the work hardening behavior of the ferrite-based duplex lightweight steel.

4. Discussion

[BLOCK b0051]
4.1. Identification of dislocation types in ferrite of 8Al and 10Al steel

[BLOCK b0052]
There are significant differences in the micro-deformation behavior in ferrite of 10Al steel and 8Al steel, which are closely related to the activation of different types of dislocations. Here, the two-beam diffraction mode has been employed to investigate the types of dislo cations in ferrite of 10Al steel and 8Al steel. To better observe the dis locations, the samples are pre-deformed to the 3 % engineering strain. Fig. 7(a–f) presents the dislocation structures in ferrite of 10Al steel using the different g diffraction vectors near the [111] zone axis.

[BLOCK b0053]
To identify the Burgers vector b of the ferrite, the g⋅b analyses are conducted. When g = [10 1] and $g = [ 1 \overline { { 1 } } 0 ]$ operating vector, some long straight dislocations systematically lose contrast (Fig. 7(a and b)), whereas when $g = [ 0 \mathrm { ~ \overline { { 1 } } ~ }$ 1] operating vector, all dislocations are visible (Fig. 7(c)). It can be concluded that the Burgers vector b is determined to be $a / 2 [ 1 1 1 ]$ by the $g \cdot b = 0$ analysis of the ferrite in 10Al steel, as shown in Fig. 7(a–c). Similarly, the Burgers vector of dislocations in ferrite of 8Al steel is also analyzed near the [111] zone axis, as shown in Fig. 7 $( { \pmb g } - { \bf l } ) .$ . Some dislocations systematically lost contrast under the [0 1 1] and [1 10] operating vector conditions. Subsequently, the Burgers vec tor b is also determined to be a/2[111] through the g⋅b = 0 analysis of the ferrite in 8Al steel.

[BLOCK b0054]
Fig. 7(a–l) shows that the dislocation structures are quite different although the Burgers vectors of dislocations in ferrite of both 10Al steel and 8Al steel are of the $a / 2 < 1 1 1 > \mathrm { t y p e . A }$ predominant fraction of the dislocations in the ferrite of 10Al steel are identified as long straight screw dislocations, and their distribution is non-uniform, as shown in Fig. 7(a–f). These long straight screw dislocations are commonly observed in Fe-Al ordered alloys [14–16,48], which can be attributed at least partially to the significant energy difference between edge and screw dislocations, where the energy of screw dislocations is notably lower than that of edge dislocations [14].

[BLOCK b0055]
Comparing with the long straight screw dislocations in 10Al steel, a substantial number of edge dislocations and mixed dislocations with near-edge character are uniformly distributed in the ferrite phase of 8Al steel. This homogeneous distribution of dislocations can effectively mitigate brittle fracture caused by stress concentration. It is reported in conventional body-centered cubic (BCC) alloys, the gliding velocities of edge dislocations are typically much higher than those of screw dislo cations [18,19], owing to the lower resistance stress. Consequently, the edge segments are more readily annihilated within BCC alloys, leaving screw dislocations as the predominant type in the deformed micro structures [21]. However, edge dislocations have also been reported in the BCC-structured Mo-Nb-Ti multi-principal element alloy (MPEA) [49] and Ti-Zr-Hf-Nb high-entropy alloy (HEA) [20]. The local chemical fluctuations in these alloys result in relatively high glide resistance for edge dislocations on the glide plane, making it less likely for edge dis: locations to annihilate. Similarly, B2 and DO ordered domains increase the resistance stress to the glide of edge dislocations in the ferrite of 8Al steel, which reduces the glide velocity of the edge dislocations and consequently leads to a significant multiplication of edge dislocations in 8Al steel.

In contrast, the ferrite of 8Al steel produces a significant number of edge dislocations and mixed dislocations with edge character, which markedly enhances the deformation capability of the 8Al steel ferrite.

4.2. The planar slip of dislocations in ferrite

[BLOCK b0060]
In body-centered cubic (BCC) materials, screw dislocations typically exhibit pronounced cross-slip behavior due to their non-planar glide characteristics [52], which is fundamentally linked to the asymmetric core structure of screw dislocations and their high Peierls-Nabarro stress [53,54]. Interestingly, both edge and screw dislocations in 8Al steel and 10Al steel show distinct planar slip, as illustrated in Figs. 5 and 7. Studies demonstrate that the interaction between dislocations and or dered structures can lead to planar slip of dislocations, as observed in titanium alloys [30,31], FCC-structured Fe-Mn-Al-C alloys [12,13], and BCC-structured TiZrHfNbTa0.2 high-entropy alloys (HEAs) [32].

4.3. Strain hardening behavior of 8Al steel

[BLOCK b0070]
Fig. 3(d) reveals that 8Al steel exhibits multi-stage strain hardening, which is associated with the dynamic slip band refinement mechanism in ferrite. In stage A, the movement of dislocations leads to a sharp decrease in the strain hardening rate. In stage B, a reduction in the strain hardening rate is caused by the slip plane softening effect induced by dislocation shearing of the $\mathrm { D O } _ { 3 }$ long-range ordered domains [13]. In stage $\mathrm { C } ,$ the passing stress generated by slip bands of stored dislocations has been demonstrated to be the primary contributor to strain hardening [12]. With the increase of strain, the number of slip bands increases, the spacing between slip bands decreases, and the passing stress rises, which leads to an increase in the strain hardening rate. In stage D, with the continued increase of strain, the slip bands become nearly saturated, and cross-slip of dislocations is activated, which leads to a decrease in the strain hardening rate. In stage $\mathrm { E } ,$ localization of stress results in a rapid decline in the strain hardening rate and leads to plastic failure.

[BLOCK b0071]
The deformation structures of ferrite and austenite in 8Al steel are dominated by dislocations until a small amount of deformation twins are activated in austenite near the maximum force. Therefore, the excellent strain hardening performance of 8Al steel is attributed to the refinement of ferrite slip bands and austenite dislocation cells. In the subsequent calculations, the flow stress of 8Al steel is estimated using the average spacing of ferrite slip bands and the average dislocation cell size of austenite. In dislocation-mediated plastic materials, the total flow stress $\sigma _ { t o t }$ can be expressed by the following equation [57,58]:

[BLOCK b0072]
$$
\sigma_ {t o t} = \sigma_ {0} + \sigma (\varepsilon)\tag{5}
$$

[BLOCK b0073]
where $\sigma _ { 0 }$ represents the deformation-independent strengthening contribution, i.e., the yield stress, and the strain hardening term $\sigma ( \varepsilon )$ characterizes the evolution of flow stress during plastic deformation. The strain hardening term σ(ε) is composed of two parts, ferrite and austenite, and can be expressed as follows:

[BLOCK b0074]
$$
\sigma (\varepsilon) = 0. 7 5 7 \sigma_ {\alpha} (\varepsilon) + 0. 2 4 3 \sigma_ {\gamma} (\varepsilon)\tag{6}
$$

[BLOCK b0075]
where 75.7 % and 24.3 % represent the mass fractions of ferrite (α) and austenite (γ), respectively, and $\sigma _ { \alpha } ( \varepsilon )$ and $\sigma _ { \gamma } ( \varepsilon )$ denote the strain hard ening terms for ferritic and austenitic phases during plastic deformation.

[BLOCK b0076]
The ferrite strain hardening contribution from slip band refinement is determined through passing stress analysis [12,59]:

[BLOCK b0077]
$$
\sigma_ {(\alpha)} (\varepsilon) = K M G b / D\tag{7}
$$

[BLOCK b0078]
where K is a geometrical factor, $M = 2 . 8 \ [ 5 6 , 6 0 ]$ is the Taylor-factor, G = 80.7 GPa [56] is the shear modulus, b = 0.25 nm [56] is the magnitude of the Burgers vector, and D is the mean slip band spacing (Fig. 6).

[BLOCK b0079]
The austenite strain hardening contribution arising from slip band, dislocation cell block, and dislocation cell refinement is derived from an empirical relationship [45,46,61]:

$$

$$

Where K is the similitude coefficient, M is the Taylor factor $( M _ { S B } = M _ { D C B }$ = 3.06, M = 2.44) [46], G = 70 GPa [46] denotes the shear modulus, b = 0.25 nm [62] represents the magnitude of the Burgers vector, and D corresponds to the slip band spacing, dislocation cell block size, or dislocation cell size (Fig. 6). The values 0.8 and 0.2 are the area fractions of the dislocation cell blocks and the dislocation cells, respectively.

5. Conclusion

[BLOCK b0087]
The initial microstructure, mechanical properties, and deformation microstructure evolution of Fe-35Mn-xAl-0.1C (x = 4, 6, 8, 10 wt%) lowcarbon lightweight steels were investigated.
