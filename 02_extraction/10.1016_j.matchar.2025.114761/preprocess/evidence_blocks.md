A B S T R A C T

[BLOCK b0008]
Low-density steels have garnered significant attention due to the demand for lightweight structural materials in aerospace and automobile industries. However, manufacturing low-density steels with different hardness levels poses challenges in composition design, processing optimization, and the application of hardening techniques across various alloy systems. In this work, Fe-35Mn-(15, 17, 19)Al-5Cu (at.%) low-density steels with an ultrawide flexible hardness ranging from 253.4 HV to 965.6 HV through simple aging treatments were developed, attributing to the continuous precipitation of the ultra-hard β-Mn phase. The densities of the Fe-Mn-Al-Cu steels were regulated by varying the Al contents, and the effects of Al contents on the aging hardening behaviors of Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels were systematically investigated. The results show that the increase of Al content led to the decrease in precipitation kinetics of β-Mn. Moreover, increasing Al content slightly enhanced the intrinsic hardness of β-Mn. However. it also led to the decrease of FCC Cu-rich phase between β-Mn interfaces and the decrease in the fracture toughness of the ultra-hard alloys. This resulted in the decrease in the final hardening effect of Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels with increasing Al content. Due to the advantages of ultra-high hardness (965.6 HV) and low density, our Fe-35.2Mn-15Al-5.2Cu low-density steel achieved an ultra-high specific hardness of 141 HV⋅cm<sup>3</sup>/g.

[BLOCK b0009]
1. Introduction

[BLOCK b0010]
In recent years, low-density steel, also known as lightweight steel, has garnered widespread interest due to the demand for lightweight structural materials [1–5]. Low-density steels refer to a class of steels with a lower density compared to traditional steels, typically achieved through alloying design [6–8]. Ham and Cairns initially developed a <sub>cost-effective</sub> <sub>Fe-34Mn-10Al-0.76C</sub> <sub>low-density</sub> <sub>steel</sub> <sub>to</sub> <sub>replace</sub> <sub>Cr</sub>–<sub>Ni</sub> stainless steel in mildly corrosive environments [9]. In this steel, the Al and Mn were used as substitutes for Cr and Ni, respectively, to enhance its corrosion resistance and high-temperature oxidation resistance. Subsequent research on low-density steels primarily focused on reducing material density without significantly compromising mechanical prop erties, in order to meet the demands for high-strength lightweight metallic materials in aerospace, automotive, and other industries [10–12]. It is estimated that reducing the density of steels by just 10 % could maintain or even significantly enhance its superiority and competitiveness in industrial applications owing to their high specific strength and specific hardness [13,14]. The specific hardness and spe cific strength of low-density steels are critical in industrial applications, particularly when used as structural materials. Enhancing hardness in low-density steels offers significant benefits, especially in applications where wear resistance and long service life are critical [15–18]. How ever, further increasing the hardness of low-density steels remains a formidable challenge.

[BLOCK b0011]
Low-density steels primarily consist of binary Fe-Al, Fe-Mn-Al, and Fe-Mn-Si-Al alloys [7,8,19]. Among them, Fe-Mn-Al alloys are highly promising in engineering applications thanks to their outstanding me chanical properties and reduced specific weight. Additionally, Fe-Mn-Al alloys have been reported to possess several appealing advantages, such as high strength and toughness at room and low temperatures [20,21], excellent fatigue properties [22], and good oxidation resistance at elevated temperatures [23]. Notably, Fe-Mn-Al low-density steels exhibit remarkable aging hardening capabilities, allowing for a significant increase in hardness through heat treatments. This is pri marily attributed to the precipitation of the ultra-hard β-Mn during the aging process [24–26]. However, previous research indicates that the hardening effect of β-Mn precipitated in Fe-Mn-Al low-density steels is still limited, with the maximum hardness typically falling below 800 HV [24,25]. Furthermore, the slow precipitation rate of β-Mn during aging would extend the aging time for achieving high hardness. For instance, the maximum hardness value achieved in Fe-31Mn-11Al-0.89C steel was 750 HV after aging at $5 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for over 1000 min [24]. This limitation is not only due to the slow precipitation rate of β-Mn but also related to its insufficient formation amount. Therefore, further optimizing the pre cipitation behavior to promote the precipitation of β-Mn in Fe-Mn-Al low-density steels during the aging process is crucial for further enhancing the hardness of Fe-Mn-Al low-density steels. This could further broaden the application range of Fe-Mn-Al low-density steels and save production costs and time.

We have previously developed an Fe-Mn-Al-Cu alloy with rapid aging hardening response and ultra-high hardness [15]. The addition of Cu not only promoted the precipitation kinetics of β-Mn, but also enhanced the hardness of Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steel. Similarly, Al plays an important role not only in the density and mechanical prop erties of low-density steels, but also possibly in the precipitation and hardening behavior of β-Mn in the Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels dur ing aging treatments. In this work, we investigated the effects of Al contents on the aging hardening behaviors of Fe-35Mn-xAl-5Cu (at.%, x = 15, 17, 19) low-density steels based on considering the dual criteria of low density and high hardness. Firstly, high Mn content of 35 at.% was chosen to promote the precipitation kinetics of hard β-Mn, mainly responsible for the achieved ultrahigh and flexible hardness. In addition, the addition of different Al contents is due to the following consider ations: 1) Al is the primary element responsible for reducing the density in Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels. The addition of 1 wt.% Al reduces the density by approximately 0.1 $g / { \mathrm { c m } } ^ { 3 }$ [21,27]. 2) The effect of Al on the precipitation behavior of β-Mn during the aging process of Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels has yet to be thoroughly explored. Through aging treatments between $5 2 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and $6 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ of no more than 10 h, we ach ieved an exceptionally wide and adjustable range of hardness in these Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels, spanning from 253.4 HV to 965.6 HV. This was primarily attributed to the rapid precipitation of the hard β-Mn phase. In addition, an ultra-high specific hardness of 141 HV⋅cm<sup>3</sup>/g were achieved in the Fe-35.2Mn-15Al-5.2Cu low-density steel. These findings pave a new way for developing low-density steels with ultrahigh and flexible hardness within a wide range.

2. Materials and methods

[BLOCK b0016]
2.1. Alloy preparation

[BLOCK b0017]
The alloy ingots with the nominal compositions of Fe-35Mn-xAl-5Cu (at.%, x = 15, 17, 19) were produced through melting raw materials with high purity (>99.9 %) using a vacuum induction furnace under an argon atmosphere. The alloys are referred to as 15Al, 17Al, and 19Al according to their Al contents, respectively. The chemical compositions measured via Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) are detailed in Table 1. The densities of 15Al, 17Al and 19Al alloys were measured by Archimedes method as 6.85, 6.76, $6 . 6 6 ~ \mathrm { g } { \cdot } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 3 }$ . The ingots were ho mogenized at 1200 <sup>◦</sup>C for 24 h, then hot-rolled into plates with a thickness of 4 mm. Subsequently. these plates were cut into $1 0 \times 5 \times 2$ mm<sup>3</sup> samples using electrical discharge machining for Vickers hardness measurements and microstructure characterizations. The samples were solution treated at $1 1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 10 min under an argon atmosphere, followed by oil quenching. The solution-treated samples were then aged at temperatures between 525 <sup>◦</sup>C and 650 <sup>◦</sup>C for 1, 3, 6 and 10 h, followed by water quenching. Samples for Vickers hardness tests, X-Ray Diffrac tion (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and electron back scattered diffraction (EBSD) characterizations were prepared by electrolytically polishing using a mixture of 10 % perchloric acid and 90 % ethanol. Samples for transmission electron microscope (TEM) obser vation were first mechanically ground to thin foils with thicknesses of about 90 μm, then punched into foils with diameters of 3 mm. Finally, the TEM foils were ion thinned.

[BLOCK b0020]
The Vickers hardness of the samples was measured using an HVS-1000 digital microhardness tester with a load of 9.8 N for 15 s. Each sample was repeatedly tested 5 times, and the hardness values were given by the average of 5 measurements. Samples for nanoindentation tests were mechanically polished to prevent the Cu-rich phase from flaking due to electrolytic polishing. Nanoindentation tests were conducted using an Agilent G200 Nanoindenter. The indentation depth was 200 nm, and each test point was spaced 5 μm or more apart.

3.2. Evolutions of constituent phases after different aging treatments

[BLOCK b0040]
The EBSD results depicted in Fig. 3 demonstrate that a single α (Body-centered cubic, BCC) phase formed in the solution-treated Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels. To clarify the microstructural origin of the rapid aging hardening behaviors of Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels, EBSD characterization was performed on these alloys subjected to different aging treatments. Fig. 4 presents the EBSD results of the 15Al alloy after different aging treatments. After aging at 525 <sup>◦</sup>C for 3 h, it is evident that a significant amount of β-Mn had precipitated (85.8 %), with only a small amount of α matrix remaining untransformed. The formation of β-Mn led to the increase in hardness from 253.4 HV at the solid-solution state to 872.0 HV, indicating the strong hardening effect of the β-Mn phase. After aging at $5 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 6 h, the α matrix had almost entirely transformed into β-Mn. Furthermore, a new fine BCC phase was present within the β-Mn. Following aging at 650 <sup>◦</sup>C for 3 h, the un transformed α regions were no longer observed, and a new FCC phase was distributed in β-Mn.

It is noteworthy that in the sample aged at a high temperature of 650 <sup>◦</sup>C for 6 h, the α matrix had almost completely transformed into β-Mn, and a great amount of new BCC phase was observed within the β-Mn.

Besides, some fine particles are evenly distributed at the interfaces between the β-Mn. The selected area elec tron diffraction (SAED) patterns shown in Fig. 11 (b) indicate that the fine particles exhibited an FCC crystalline structure, with FCC twins frequently observed within the particles. [BLOCK b0059]
3.4. Intrinsic hardness of β-Mn

[BLOCK b0060]
Fig. 13 presents the nanoindentation results for the hardest samples of the three Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels after aging treatments. The peak Vickers hardness values for the 15Al, 17Al, and 19Al alloys were 965.6 HV, 924.9 HV, and 917.8 HV, respectively. As shown in Fig. 13 (a), the average hardness values of the same samples measured by nanoindentation were $1 1 . 7 \pm 0 . 4 \ : \mathrm { G P a } , 1 1 . 1 \pm 0 . 6 \ : \mathrm { G P a } ,$ and $1 0 . 4 \pm 1 . 3$ GPa, respectively. Both the peak Vickers hardness and micro-hardness decreased with increasing Al content. The presence of the FCC Cu-rich and BCC phases resulted in variations in micro-hardness values across different areas, as shown in Fig. 13 (b). The micro-hardness decreased when the indenter was pressed into the FCC Cu-rich and BCC phases, indicating that the intrinsic hardness of these precipitates were all lower than that of the β-Mn. The error in micro-hardness increased with increasing Al content, which laterally indicates that the content of these FCC and BCC precipitates also increased with the increase in Al content. We selected the areas where the nanoindentation indents did not contact the FCC Cu-rich and BCC phases to calculate the intrinsic hardness of the β-Mn. Accordingly, the β-Mn in hardest samples of 15Al, 17Al, and 19Al alloys showed the average intrinsic hardnesses of $1 2 . 1 \pm 0 . 3 \mathrm { G P a } ,$ 12.2 ± 0.1 GPa, and 12.5 ± 0.4 GPa, respectively. Because of the high intrinsic hardness of β-Mn, the alloys exhibited rapid hardening response during the aging treatments and achieved ultra-high hardness. Additionally, the chemical compositions of β-Mn in the three samples were measured by SME-EDS, and the results are shown in Fig. 14. In the hardest samples of 15Al, 17Al, and 19Al alloys, the Al contents in the β-Mn were 14.3 at.%, 16.3 at.%, and 17.5 at.%, respectively. However, the contents of other elements in the three allovs were very close to each other. These results indicate that the increase in Al content slightly enhanced the intrinsic hardness of β-Mn in 15Al, 17Al, and 19Al alloys through solid-solution strengthening. However, as can be seen in Fig. 13 (b), the FCC Cu-rich and BCC phases between the β-Mn interfaces gradually increased with increasing Al content. Since the intrinsic hardness of these precipitates was lower than the intrinsic hardness of β-Mn, in this case, it resulted in the overall hardness showing a pattern of decreasing with increasing Al content.

[BLOCK b0061]
Since the ultra-high hardness would result in a decrease in the toughness, cracks were generated during the Vickers hardness testing. The average crack lengths resulting from the hardness measurements were 18 μm for 15Al alloy, 22 μm for 17Al alloy, and 35 μm for 19Al alloy. Therefore, the calculated $K _ { \mathrm { I C } }$ values by using Eq. (1) for the hardest samples of the 15Al, 17Al, and 19Al alloys were 8.6 MPa $\mathbf { m } ^ { 1 / 2 } ,$ ${ 6 . 6 \ \mathrm { M P a } } { \cdot } { \mathrm { m } } ^ { 1 / 2 }$ , and $3 . 5 \ \mathrm { M P a } { \cdot } \mathrm { m } ^ { 1 / 2 }$ , respectively. This indicates that the $K _ { \mathrm { I C } }$ of ultra-hard Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels decreased with increasing Al content.

4. Discussions

[BLOCK b0064]
4.1. Effect of Al contents on aging hardening behaviors of Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels

[BLOCK b0065]
The combined microstructure observations and nanoindentation re sults indicated that the key to achieving ultra-high hardness in Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels through aging lies in the complete phase transformation from the α matrix to β-Mn. In this study. we investigated the effects of Al contents on the aging hardening responses of Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels. The results showed that the increase in Al contents decreased the precipitation kinetics of β-Mn. When the Al content was 15.0 at.%, β-Mn precipitated rapidly during the aging processes at temperatures of $5 2 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and above. For example, within 3 h of aging at 525 ${ } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ the α matrix had been almost completely transformed into β-Mn (Fig. 7). However, for the 17Al and 19Al alloys, this transformation was only completed after 6 h and 10 h when aging at $5 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ respectively (Figs. 8 and 9). Both the precipitation and growth of β-Mn involve the process of atomic diffusion. The decrease in the precipitation kinetics of β-Mn with increasing Al contents is explained as follows: 1) The β-Mn is a Mn-rich phase, and its nucleation and growth must exclude large amounts of other atoms to the surroundings. As shown in Fig. 15, the β-Mn is rich in Mn while poor in Al and Cu. In this case, the increase in Al content resulted in the enrichment of more Al atoms around β-Mn, which would hinder its further precipitation and growth. 2) In Fe-Mn-Albased alloys with high Al contents, the precipitation of the $\mathrm { D } 0 _ { 3 }$ phase after solution treatment and quenching is quite common $[ 3 0 , 3 1 ]$ . The increase in Al content will lead to an increase in the amount and size of the $\mathrm { D } 0 _ { 3 }$ phase in Fe-Mn-Al-based alloys, attributing to the strong stabi lizing effect of the Al element on the Al-rich $\mathrm { D } 0 _ { 3 }$ phase [32,33]. In our previous study, the precipitation of the $\mathrm { D } 0 _ { 3 }$ phase in α-matrix was observed in both Fe-37.8Mn-6.9Al and Fe-36.5Mn $6 . 5 \mathrm { A l } { - 7 . 7 \mathrm { C u } }$ (wt.%) alloys after solution treatment. Moreover, the $\mathrm { D } 0 _ { 3 }$ phase would continue to precipitate and grow during the subsequent aging treatment [15]. However, the $\mathrm { D } 0 _ { 3 }$ phase was not observed in alloys with the maximum hardness, which suggests that the precipitation and growth of β-Mn were accompanied by the depletion of the $\mathrm { D } 0 _ { 3 }$ phase. Since the increase in Al content would lead to an increase in the amount and size of the $\mathrm { D } 0 _ { 3 }$ phase and also stabilize it, this might hinder the the nucleation and growth of β-Mn due to the prerequisite of the depletion of the $\mathrm { D } 0 _ { 3 }$ phase. The increase in aging temperature accelerated the precipitation and growth of β-Mn, a phenomenon attributed to the enhanced driving force for β-Mn formation and the accelerated atomic diffusion rate [15]. The increase in the volume fraction of β-Mn significantly enhanced the hardness, as the β-Mn exhibited an intrinsic high hardness of over 10 GPa [24].

The contents of Al also exerted a significant influence on the mi crostructures of ultra-hard alloys. In the alloys with peak hardness value, new BCC phase and FCC Cu-rich phase formed at the interfaces between the β-Mn. Although only the FCC Cu-rich phase was characterized in the TEM results (Figs. 11 and 12), the EBSD results (Figs. 4, 5, and 6) clearly revealed the presence of the BCC phase within the β-Mn. Meanwhile. the results of our previous study also confirmed the existence of the BCC phase [15]. It is evident that the size distribution of the BCC phase particles is comparable to that of the FCC Cu-rich phase. As the β-Mn is an Mn-rich phase, its nucleation and growth involve the diffusion and redistribution of elements, which enriched Mn while expelling $\mathrm { F e } , \mathrm { A l } ,$ and Cu into the surrounding matrix. Therefore, the formation of the BCC phase at the interfaces between the β-Mn is attributed to the enrichment of BCC stabilizing element Al around the interfaces of β-Mn [25]. The Cu-rich phase was formed due to the limited solubility of Cu in Fe-based alloys and the expelling of Cu from the β-Mn during its precipitation and growth [15,34]. The microstructure evolutions during aging treatments of the Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels followed this sequence: ${ \mathfrak { x } } \to { \mathfrak { x } } +$ FCC Cu-rich phase $- \alpha \mathbf { - } \mathbf { M } \mathbf { n } + \beta \mathbf { - } \mathbf { M } \mathbf { n } + \mathbf { B } \mathbf { C } \mathbf { C }$ phase→ β-Mn + BCC phase + FCC Cu-rich phase. The precipitates between β-Mn interfaces in the ultra-hard alloys gradually increase with the increase in Al content (Fig. 13). Furthermore, the EBSD results (Figs. 4, 5 and 6) show that the content of the FCC Cu-rich phase between β-Mn interfaces decreased gradually with the increase of the Al content.

[BLOCK b0069]
According to the nanoindentation results, the increase in Al content would lead to the decrease in the micro-hardness of ultra-hard Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels (Fig. 13). Although the addition of Al enhanced the intrinsic hardness of the β-Mn through solid-solution strengthening, this enhancement was minimal as reflected by a marginal increase in the intrinsic hardness of the β-Mn [29]. Besides, the increase in Al content led to the increase of the content of FCC Cu-rich phase and BCC phase between the β-Mn interfaces. It is evident that both the intrinsic hard nesses of FCC Cu-rich and BCC phase were all significantly lower than that of β-Mn. The ultra-hard Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels were composed of β-Mn, as well as the FCC Cu-rich and BCC phases distrib uted between their interfaces. As a result, the hardness depended not only on the intrinsic hardness of β-Mn, but also on the absolute volume fraction of β-Mn. Elevated Al content led to the increase of FCC Cu-rich phase and BCC phase between the β-Mn interfaces, resulting in the decrease in the absolute volume fraction of β-Mn. Taking the EBSD re sults of the three alloys aged at $6 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ as an example, the volume fraction of FCC Cu-rich and BCC phases of the 15Al alloy after aging at 650 <sup>◦</sup>C for 3 h was $3 . 9 \% ,$ and the Vickers hardness of this sample was 852.3 HV. The volume fraction of these precipitates of the 17Al alloy after aging at $6 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 10 h was $4 . 7 ~ \% ,$ and the average Vickers hardness of the alloy was 833.2 HV. However, after the 19Al alloy aged at $6 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 6 h, the volume fraction of FCC Cu-rich and BCC pre cipitates reached 6.5 %, and its Vickers hardness was only 810.9 HV. Ultimately. this led to the decrease in the overall micro-hardness as well as the highest Vickers hardness value of Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels with increasing Al content. In addition, the $K _ { \mathrm { I C } }$ of Fe-Mn-Al-Cu lowdensity steels decreased with increasing Al content. Firstly, the intrinsic hardness of β-Mn gradually increased with increasing Al content. and the $K _ { \mathrm { I C } }$ should be deteriorating accordingly. Moreover, the FCC phase exhibited relatively low intrinsic hardness but is more ductile, while the BCC phase is usually harder than the FCC phase [35]. Therefore, the decrease in ductile FCC Cu-rich phase due to increasing Al content led to the decrease in fracture toughness of ultra-hard alloys. The above two reasons together contributed to the decrease in the $K _ { \mathrm { I C } }$ of Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels with increasing Al content.

4.2. Insights into Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels

[BLOCK b0073]
In this study, we achieved exceptionally wide adjustable hardness values, ranging from 253.4 HV to 965.6 HV in the Fe-Mn-Al-Cu lowdensity steels through simple aging treatments. Notably, different hardness levels were obtained in these alloys by controlling aging pa rameters, thereby meeting various practical application requirements. Fig. 16 compares the specific hardness (hardness/density, HV/ρ) of our Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels with various low-density steels, other types of steels, pure iron, and medium/high entropy alloys (M/HEAs) [17,18,35–73]. The Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels not only exhibit ultra-high hardness across a wide range of flexibility but also boast the advantage of low density. The hardness of existing steels and M/HEAs generally does not exceed 800 HV and coupled with the higher densities. This results in their specific hardness usually below $1 2 0 \ { \mathrm { H V } } { \cdot } { \mathrm { c m } } ^ { 3 } / { \mathrm { g } } .$ In contrast, our Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels achieve a maximum spe cific hardness exceeding 140 HV⋅cm<sup>3</sup>/g while maintaining low density. Furthermore, the Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels with ultra-high hard ness also demonstrate acceptable fracture toughness. For instance, the 15Al alloy maintains a fracture toughness of 8.6 MPa⋅m<sup>1/2</sup> even at a high hardness level of 965.5 HV. This represents a significant breakthrough in the research on high-performance low-density steels.

[BLOCK b0074]
Ultra-high hardness over 950 HV was achieved in Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low density steels with high Al contents. After the simple short-time aging treatments, the continuous precipitation of β-Mn led to the achievement of an ultra-wide range of flexible and tunable hardness. This means that these alloys can be formed into parts or components with different shapes and sizes in the soft state (solution-treated) and then aged to obtain the appropriate hardness range, which could significantly reduce the preparation cost and difficulty of traditional high hardness and wear-resistant components. In the alloys studied in this work, the in crease in Al content would slow down the precipitation rate of β-Mn and decrease the fracture toughness of the alloys with peak hardness. Therefore, the change in the mechanical performance of Fe-Mn-Al-based low-density steels needs to be considered comprehensively when reducing their density. In summary, our Fe-Mn-Al-Cu alloys are able to increase the hardness dramatically within short-time aging, saving fabrication time and costs. In addition, its low density and flexible hardness can realize “one type of material, multiple uses” under the condition of meeting the application premise of lightweight structural materials. Despite the superior specific hardness of these Fe-Mn-Al-Cu alloys, the toughness remains not good enough. Therefore, achieving an optimal combination between hardness and toughness warrants further study.

[BLOCK b0075]
5. Conclusions

[BLOCK b0076]
In this work, we systematically investigated the effects of Al contents on the aging hardening behaviors of the Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels subjected to different aging treatments and derived the following conclusions:

[BLOCK b0077]
1) An ultra-wide flexible hardness ranging from 253.4 HV to 965.6 HV was tailored in Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels with different Al contents. Due to the combination of ultra-high hardness and lowdensity advantages, the Fe-35.2Mn-15.0Al-5.2Cu alloy achieved an ultrahigh specific hardness of 141 HV⋅cm<sup>3</sup>/g.

[BLOCK b0078]
2) The increase in Al contents slowed down the precipitation kinetics of β-Mn during aging treatments, which led to the complete trans formation from α to β-Mn being extended.

[BLOCK b0079]
3) The increase in Al content slightly enhanced the intrinsic hardness of β-Mn. However, it also led to the decrease of FCC Cu-rich phase between β-Mn interfaces and the decrease in the fracture toughness of the ultra-hard alloys. This resulted in the decrease in the final hardening effect of Fe-Mn-Al-Cu low-density steels with increasing Al content.
